# 0. Executive decision

## The proposition

Build a trusted, beautifully edited **knowledge and discovery platform
about Rwanda**. It should help people understand the country through its
places, landscapes, history, heritage, culture, communities and
experiences.

It is not simply a tourism website, a travel agency, a user-generated
review directory or a Wikipedia clone.

It is a curated national knowledge product with a geographical
interface:

-   **Knowledge is the foundation:** reliable, sourced, contextual
    information.
-   **Discovery is the experience:** make it easy to find places,
    stories and connections.
-   **The atlas is the organising system:** geography connects the
    information.
-   **Travel utility is a layer:** practical visitor information is
    included when verified and useful.
-   **Commerce is optional and clearly separated:** referrals, bookings
    or sponsorship must never compromise editorial independence.

## Strategic recommendation

Use the combined model:

1.  A place-and-story discovery experience as the public entry point.
2.  A structured knowledge library as the content engine.
3.  A map and geographic taxonomy as the organising layer.

The platform should feel like a modern cultural institution with the
clarity of a well-designed reference tool---not a crowded tourism
marketplace.

## One-sentence description

**A trusted guide to understanding Rwanda---through the places, people,
history and stories that shape it.**

## Working tagline

**Know the place. Understand the story.**

Alternative lines to test: - Rwanda, explored and explained. - A
country, more deeply known. - Discover the places. Understand their
stories.

Do not commit to a tagline until audience testing and brand-name
validation.

# 1. Foundation

## 1.1 The problem

Information about Rwanda is distributed across tourism sites, government
portals, park operators, museums, publications, academic sources, local
organisations, maps and social platforms. The user must often move
between sources to understand a place fully.

The platform should address five related problems:

1.  **Fragmentation:** useful information is scattered.
2.  **Shallow descriptions:** places are often described as attractions
    without explaining why they matter.
3.  **Uneven discoverability:** lesser-known places and local knowledge
    can be hard to find.
4.  **Weak connections:** a place, its history, nearby locations and
    wider cultural or environmental context are rarely connected in one
    experience.
5.  **Trust and currency:** practical details can become outdated, while
    historical and cultural claims require careful sourcing and context.

These are product hypotheses, not yet proven market findings. Validate
them through interviews, search-intent research, content audits and
prototype tests before investing heavily.

## 1.2 The opportunity

Create a single, navigable system where users can:

-   Search for a known place.
-   Browse Rwanda without knowing what to search for.
-   Understand a place's historical, cultural, geographical or
    ecological significance.
-   Find related places and stories.
-   Access practical visit information when it is available and
    verified.
-   Follow sources and distinguish established facts from
    interpretation.
-   Learn about Rwanda without necessarily planning a trip.

## 1.3 Mission, vision and purpose

**Mission**\
Make reliable knowledge about Rwanda easier to discover, understand and
use.

**Vision**\
Become one of the most trusted and useful digital references for
understanding and exploring Rwanda.

**Purpose**\
To document the country's places and stories with accuracy, care and
clarity, and make that knowledge accessible to people in Rwanda and
around the world.

## 1.4 Product principles

1.  **Truth before traffic.** Never trade accuracy for clicks.
2.  **Useful before abundant.** A smaller verified collection is better
    than a huge weak directory.
3.  **Context before promotion.** Explain why a place matters before
    asking anyone to visit.
4.  **Clarity before decoration.** Every visual and interface element
    must serve a purpose.
5.  **Local knowledge matters.** Work with people and institutions
    connected to the places being documented.
6.  **Respect is non-negotiable.** Handle history, identity, culture and
    sensitive sites responsibly.
7.  **No false completeness.** Clearly label what is known, unknown,
    unavailable or awaiting verification.
8.  **Editorial independence.** Paid placement must never masquerade as
    an independent recommendation.
9.  **Accessibility by default.** Make the product usable across
    devices, bandwidth conditions and abilities.
10. **Build sustainably.** Do not add features or operating costs
    without evidence of value.

# 2. Positioning and category

## 2.1 Category

**A national place-based knowledge and discovery platform.**

This category is intentionally broader than tourism and more curated
than a general encyclopedia.

## 2.2 Positioning statement

For people who want to understand or explore Rwanda, Rwanda Atlas is a
trusted digital reference and discovery platform that connects places
with their history, geography, culture, nature and practical context.
Unlike a basic tourist directory or an open-ended encyclopedia, it
organises carefully researched information around real places and makes
the connections easy to explore.

## 2.3 What it is

-   A curated reference for places and stories about Rwanda.
-   A geographical discovery tool.
-   An editorial publication with consistent standards.
-   A practical starting point for exploring destinations.
-   A long-term knowledge asset that can serve education, tourism and
    research.

## 2.4 What it is not

-   Not a travel agency.
-   Not a booking engine at launch.
-   Not a social network.
-   Not an open-edit encyclopedia at launch.
-   Not a paid-listing directory.
-   Not an official government service unless formally authorised.
-   Not a substitute for park, museum, government or emergency
    authorities.
-   Not a claim that every place in Rwanda has been documented.

## 2.5 Audience priorities

### Primary audience 1: Curious Rwandans

People who want to learn more about their country, discover places and
understand local stories.

### Primary audience 2: International visitors

Travellers researching Rwanda before or during a visit, including people
who want context beyond standard itineraries.

### Secondary audience 3: Diaspora

People with personal, cultural or family connections who want accessible
information about the country.

### Secondary audience 4: Students and educators

People looking for structured, sourced introductions to places, history,
geography and heritage.

### Secondary audience 5: Researchers and practitioners

Journalists, tourism professionals, cultural organisations, conservation
groups and researchers seeking a navigable starting point. The platform
should not present itself as a substitute for specialist research.

## 2.6 Jobs to be done

-   "I know the name of a place. Help me understand it."
-   "I have a free day in this area. What meaningful places could I
    explore?"
-   "I want to learn the history behind this landmark."
-   "I want to discover places beyond the usual tourist circuit."
-   "I need reliable basic information before visiting."
-   "I am teaching or researching a topic connected to Rwanda. Help me
    find a starting point and credible sources."
-   "I want to understand how this place connects to the wider country."

# 3. Brand strategy

## 3.1 Brand essence

**Rwanda, understood through place.**

## 3.2 Brand promise

Every published page should help a person know something meaningful,
understand why it matters and decide what to explore next.

## 3.3 Brand character

-   Informed, not academic for its own sake.
-   Curious, not sensational.
-   Calm, not cold.
-   Distinctive, not decorative.
-   Welcoming, not overly familiar.
-   Proud of Rwanda, without exaggeration or propaganda.
-   Confident, without pretending to know everything.

## 3.4 Personality boundaries

  Be           Avoid
  ------------ --------------------------
  Clear        Corporate jargon
  Curious      Clickbait
  Credible     Unsupported superlatives
  Human        Forced friendliness
  Editorial    Tourist-brochure clichés
  Respectful   Exoticising communities
  Minimal      Empty, sterile design
  Helpful      Overloaded feature sets

## 3.5 Voice and tone

**Default voice:** clear, intelligent, warm, concise and evidence-aware.

Writing rules: - Use plain English first. - Explain unfamiliar Rwandan
terms rather than deleting them. - Preserve original names and spellings
where appropriate. - Use dates and place names precisely. - Separate
documented facts from interpretation. - Avoid "hidden paradise",
"untouched", "magical", "undiscovered" and similar clichés unless a
source or context genuinely justifies the wording. - Avoid treating
communities as attractions. - Avoid presenting contested or sensitive
interpretations as settled fact. - Do not invent visitor information to
make a page look complete.

Example:

**Weak:** "Discover the magical hidden gem of Rwanda, a breathtaking
paradise full of unforgettable experiences."

**Better:** "Buhanga Eco-Park is a forest site in Northern Province
associated with Rwanda's royal history. Its cultural significance and
natural environment make it worth understanding before a visit."

The better version is specific, restrained and informative. Claims still
require sourcing before publication.

## 3.6 Naming strategy

"Rwanda Atlas" is the current working title because it communicates
geography, reference and exploration. It may be too descriptive to own
strongly as a brand.

Explore naming territories before final selection:

1.  **Atlas / geography:** map, terrain, regions, coordinates,
    landscape.
2.  **Knowledge / archive:** record, index, field notes, library,
    chronicle.
3.  **Rwanda / identity:** a distinctive name rooted in local language
    or cultural meaning, reviewed with fluent speakers and cultural
    advisors.
4.  **Discovery / perspective:** viewpoint, passage, lens, field guide.

Name evaluation criteria: - Easy to say and spell. - Meaningful in
Rwanda and internationally. - Not misleadingly official. - Distinctive
enough to protect and search. - Suitable for a knowledge platform, not
only a tour operator. - Domain and social handles available. - No
unintended meaning in Kinyarwanda, English, French or other relevant
languages.

Do not use a Kinyarwanda word as decoration. Research its meaning,
nuance, pronunciation and cultural context with competent local
speakers.

# 4. Brand identity system

The identity should be clean, minimal and functional. It should not
imitate a generic safari brand, a luxury hotel, a government portal or a
technology startup.

## 4.1 Creative direction

**Editorial cartography.**

Combine: - The precision of maps and geographic notation. - The
restraint of a contemporary publication. - The warmth of documentary
photography. - The usability of a reference library. - The clarity of a
well-made field guide.

The identity should work in a browser, on a printed field guide, on a
map, in a social post, in a museum partnership and in a simple text-only
environment.

## 4.2 Logo direction

Explore three routes during the inspiration phase:

### Route A: Wordmark first

A distinctive typographic wordmark. The name carries the identity. This
is the most minimal and easiest to deploy.

### Route B: Cartographic mark

A simple abstract mark inspired by contour lines, a coordinate point, a
map fold or connected locations. Avoid literal map silhouettes unless
exceptionally well executed.

### Route C: Editorial monogram

A restrained initial-based mark paired with a strong wordmark. Useful
for small digital contexts, but must not become a generic letter inside
a circle.

Logo requirements: - Works in one colour. - Works at favicon size. -
Works in black and white. - Does not rely on gradients. - Does not
require a slogan to be recognisable. - Has horizontal, stacked and
symbol-only configurations only if each is genuinely needed. - Avoids
clichés: gorilla silhouettes, mountain clip art, safari vehicles, flags,
map pins and multiple ornamental symbols.

## 4.3 Colour system

Start with a small, functional palette. Do not choose many accent
colours merely to make the site look lively.

**Provisional palette --- subject to contrast testing**

  Token      Suggested value Purpose
  -------- ----------------- ------------------------------------
  Ink              `#202522` Primary text and strong elements
  Paper            `#F7F6F1` Main background
  White            `#FFFFFF` Cards and high-contrast surfaces
  Forest           `#315B49` Primary accent and selected states
  Muted            `#777D76` Secondary text
  Line             `#E4E3DC` Borders and dividers
  Alert            `#A34D3E` Errors or important warnings only

Rules: - Ink and paper do most of the work. - Forest is the single main
brand accent. - Alert colour is semantic, not decorative. - Do not
colour every category differently. - Category identification should use
labels, icons and text first; colour is supplementary. - Check WCAG 2.2
AA contrast for text and controls before release. - Never communicate
status using colour alone.

These values are a starting point, not final brand approval.

## 4.4 Typography

Use a restrained two-family system, or one family if it performs well.

Suggested exploration: - **Editorial display:** a readable serif with
character for major headlines and long-form story titles. - **Interface
and body:** a highly legible sans serif for navigation, labels, metadata
and body copy.

Potential families to test: - Source Serif 4 + Inter. - Literata + IBM
Plex Sans. - Newsreader + Manrope. - A well-supported local/system
sans-serif stack for performance.

Typography rules: - Avoid ultra-light body text. - Keep line lengths
comfortable. - Use tabular numerals for coordinates, dates and
statistics where helpful. - Use a small, consistent type scale. - Do not
use all caps for long headings. - Ensure support for Kinyarwanda
diacritics and all required characters. - Test mobile rendering and font
loading performance.

## 4.5 Layout and spacing

-   Use a consistent spacing scale based on 4 or 8 px increments.
-   Establish a readable content width for articles.
-   Use wider layouts for maps and image-led discovery.
-   Use generous whitespace to create hierarchy, not to hide missing
    content.
-   Align metadata consistently.
-   Avoid excessive rounded cards.
-   Avoid decorative shadows and gradients.
-   Prefer thin dividers and strong typographic hierarchy.
-   Keep navigation shallow.

## 4.6 Photography and imagery

Photography is a major part of the identity, but images must document
rather than stereotype.

Image principles: - Use authentic, location-specific imagery. - Prefer
documentary and landscape photography over generic stock. - Show people
with dignity and appropriate consent. - Do not imply that an image
depicts a particular site if its location is uncertain. - Credit
photographers and respect licensing. - Record image location, date,
author, rights and caption where possible. - Use maps, diagrams and
archival material only with appropriate permissions. - Do not use
AI-generated imagery as evidence of a real place, event, artefact or
person. - Label illustrative or generated visuals clearly.

## 4.7 Iconography

Use one coherent icon family with consistent stroke weight. Icons should
clarify actions or categories, not decorate every heading.

Avoid: - A different emoji for every destination. - Multiple competing
icon styles. - Icons without text where meaning is not obvious. - Custom
illustration for features that do not need it.

## 4.8 Motion

Motion should explain transitions and spatial relationships. Keep it
subtle: - Short transitions. - No auto-playing background video by
default. - No parallax that harms reading or performance. - Respect
reduced-motion preferences. - Never make information inaccessible
because an animation has not completed.

## 4.9 Brand applications

The identity must be tested on: - Website header and footer. - Place
page. - Map interface. - Search results. - Article page. - Social media
post. - Email newsletter. - Downloadable field guide. - Partner or
contributor document. - Favicon and app icon. - Printed sign or small
information card.

# 5. Product architecture

## 5.1 Core product model

The primary content object is not "a tourist attraction". It is a
**place**.

A place can be: - A natural feature. - A protected area. - A town or
neighbourhood. - A museum. - A historical site. - A monument or
memorial. - A cultural landscape. - A religious or community site. - An
architectural landmark. - An island, lake, mountain, forest, cave or
waterfall.

A place may connect to: - Stories. - Historical events. - People and
organisations. - Cultural practices. - Ecosystems and species. - Nearby
places. - Experiences. - Sources and records.

## 5.2 Primary navigation

Keep the public navigation to four main destinations:

1.  **Explore** --- map, regions, categories and place discovery.
2.  **Stories** --- editorial narratives and explainers.
3.  **Knowledge** --- curated reference topics, history, culture, nature
    and geography.
4.  **About** --- mission, editorial policy, sources, corrections and
    contribution.

Search should be globally accessible. "Plan a visit" can appear
contextually on place pages rather than becoming a fifth top-level
section at launch.

## 5.3 Information architecture

## 5.4 Geographic taxonomy

Use Rwanda's administrative geography consistently and maintain a
controlled data model: - Country - Province or City of Kigali -
District - Sector - Cell / village where appropriate and publicly
suitable

Do not assume all visitor-facing locations have a clean one-to-one
administrative mapping. Some sites span boundaries or are known by
different local names. Store coordinates and alternate names alongside
administrative fields.

## 5.5 Category taxonomy

Keep the initial top-level categories manageable:

-   Nature and wildlife
-   History and heritage
-   Culture and community
-   Museums and memorials
-   Cities and places
-   Experiences and activities

Categories may overlap. A place can have multiple tags, but it should
have one primary category for navigation and editorial consistency.

## 5.6 Search

Search should support: - Place names. - Alternative and historical
names. - District and region. - Topic and category. - Story titles. -
Common spelling variations. - Kinyarwanda and English terms, with
additional languages as capacity grows.

Search results should show why an item matched. Do not show empty
filters or pretend to support languages and search features that have
not been tested.

## 5.7 Map experience

The map is a discovery tool, not a decorative hero.

MVP requirements: - Pan and zoom. - Place markers with clear
categories. - Search and filters. - List/map alternative for
accessibility. - Select a marker to preview a place. - Open the full
place page. - Clear attribution and map-data licensing. - Useful
fallback when map tiles fail or bandwidth is poor.

Do not build route optimisation, live navigation or turn-by-turn
directions in the first release. Link to a trusted mapping/navigation
service when appropriate.

## 5.8 Place profile template

Every place profile should use a consistent editorial template.

**Required core fields** - Official or preferred name. - Alternate names
and spelling variants. - Place type and primary category. - Geographic
location. - Coordinates, when reliable. - Short summary. - Why the place
matters. - Main descriptive content. - Sources. - Editorial status. -
Last reviewed date.

**Conditional fields** - Historical background. - Cultural context. -
Ecological significance. - Visitor access. - Opening hours. - Admission
fees. - Contact details. - Accessibility. - Safety guidance. - Nearby
places. - Photography and media credits.

Only show conditional sections when meaningful, verified information
exists. Do not display empty headings, "N/A" fields or placeholder text
publicly.

## 5.9 Content states

Each content record must have an explicit state: - Draft - Researching -
Fact-checking - Ready for editorial review - Approved - Published -
Needs review - Archived

Public pages should never expose internal workflow labels except for a
carefully worded "information being verified" notice when useful.

## 5.10 Editorial labels

Use simple labels: - Verified information - Updated on \[date\] -
Visitor details not confirmed - Historical interpretation - Community
contribution - Sponsored information

Do not create trust badges that imply more review than actually
happened.

# 6. Content strategy and knowledge standards

## 6.1 Content pillars

1.  **Places:** the core database of locations.
2.  **Stories:** human-readable narratives that add context.
3.  **Knowledge:** structured explainers and reference topics.
4.  **Practical guidance:** access, timing, costs and visitor
    information where verified.
5.  **Connections:** related places, events, themes and geographic
    clusters.

## 6.2 Content quality hierarchy

Prioritise: 1. Primary sources and official records. 2. Relevant public
institutions and site operators. 3. Peer-reviewed research and reputable
academic publications. 4. Established museums, conservation
organisations and cultural institutions. 5. Reputable journalism and
specialist publications. 6. First-hand interviews and community
knowledge, properly attributed and contextualised.

A source's official status does not automatically make every claim
complete or neutral. Use judgement, corroboration and context.

## 6.3 Source record

For every factual claim that matters, maintain: - Claim or fact
supported. - Source title. - Publisher or author. - URL or archival
reference. - Publication date, if known. - Access date. - Relevant
passage/page. - Source type. - Reviewer. - Confidence or unresolved
caveat.

## 6.4 Historical and sensitive content

Rwanda's history requires exceptional care, especially the 1994 Genocide
against the Tutsi.

Policy: - Use historically accurate and respectful terminology. - Follow
recognised Rwandan and relevant scholarly/institutional sources. - Avoid
sensational imagery and graphic content. - Do not flatten complex events
into simplistic timelines. - Distinguish evidence, testimony,
interpretation and memory. - Consult qualified historians and relevant
institutions. - Provide context and appropriate content notices. - Do
not allow unmoderated public edits to sensitive historical pages. -
Correct errors transparently and preserve a record of significant
changes.

## 6.5 Cultural content

-   Do not treat cultural practices as frozen or uniform.
-   Explain regional, generational and community variation.
-   Avoid presenting one person's account as universal.
-   Seek community review for sensitive or sacred knowledge.
-   Respect restrictions on sacred, private or culturally sensitive
    material.
-   Do not publish knowledge simply because it can be collected.

## 6.6 Community contributions

At launch, accept corrections and suggestions through a moderated form
or email. Do not allow unrestricted public publishing.

Contribution form fields: - Name (optional where appropriate). - Contact
details (only if follow-up is needed). - Place or topic. - Suggested
correction or information. - Source or evidence. - Relationship to the
place or knowledge. - Permission to contact. - Consent and privacy
notice.

Have a clear policy for attribution, consent, sensitive information and
removal requests.

## 6.7 Content production workflow

``` text
Topic selection
    |
Research and source collection
    |
Draft place record / story
    |
Fact-checking
    |
Local or subject review where needed
    |
Editorial approval
    |
Publish
    |
Scheduled review
    |
Correction / update / archive
```

## 6.8 Editorial checklist

Before publishing: - Is the place correctly identified? - Are names and
locations checked? - Are key claims sourced? - Is the writing
understandable? - Is historical and cultural context appropriate? - Are
images accurately captioned and licensed? - Are visitor details current
and verified? - Are uncertain facts labelled? - Are related links
useful? - Is the page accessible on mobile? - Is the page genuinely
useful without decorative filler?

## 6.9 Update cadence

Suggested starting standards: - Static historical context: review
annually or when credible new information emerges. - Opening hours and
fees: verify before publication and recheck on a defined schedule. -
Access, closures and safety notices: verify as close to publication as
possible; clearly date the information. - Contact details: periodic
confirmation. - All records: display last-reviewed date where it helps
users judge freshness.

Do not promise real-time updates unless the team has a real process to
deliver them.

# 7. UX and interface principles

## 7.1 Experience philosophy

**The interface should disappear behind the information.**

Users should know where they are, understand what they are reading and
see a clear next step without being forced through unnecessary
interactions.

## 7.2 Core journeys

### Journey A: Known-place search

Home → Search → Place result → Place profile → Related places.

### Journey B: Open discovery

Home → Explore map → Filter by interest/region → Place preview → Place
profile.

### Journey C: Learning

Home → Stories → Story → Related place → Sources.

### Journey D: Visit research

Place profile → Visitor information → Nearby places → External
navigation or official operator link.

### Journey E: Correction

Place profile → Report an issue → Submit source/evidence →
Acknowledgement → Editorial review.

## 7.3 Page types

1.  Home.
2.  Explore/map.
3.  Search results.
4.  Category or regional collection.
5.  Place profile.
6.  Story/article.
7.  Knowledge topic.
8.  About/editorial standards.
9.  Contribution/correction form.
10. Not found / error / unavailable states.

## 7.4 Homepage content order

1.  Brand and concise navigation.
2.  Clear search field.
3.  One editorially selected feature with a meaningful reason to
    explore.
4.  Explore by geography or category.
5.  Selected place collection.
6.  One or two strong stories.
7.  Map invitation.
8.  Newsletter or follow option only if there is a real publishing
    cadence.
9.  Footer with sources, policies and contact.

Do not force all six content categories, every feature, a newsletter
pop-up and multiple promotional banners onto the first screen.

## 7.5 Interface rules

-   One primary action per major section.
-   Clear page titles and breadcrumbs.
-   Search should be easy to find.
-   Filters should be useful and removable.
-   Show loading, empty, error and unavailable states.
-   Do not render empty metadata.
-   Use consistent image ratios.
-   Provide captions and credits.
-   Keep body text readable.
-   Make interactive targets large enough.
-   Ensure keyboard navigation and visible focus.
-   Support screen readers with semantic structure.
-   Respect reduced motion.
-   Provide text alternatives to maps.
-   Do not autoplay audio or video.
-   Avoid intrusive pop-ups.

## 7.6 What to remove

Remove any element that does not help the user: - Understand the
place. - Find something. - Compare relevant options. - Navigate. -
Assess trust. - Take a useful next step.

A feature that cannot be maintained should not be presented as a core
promise.

# 8. Technology and data foundations

## 8.1 Build philosophy

Start with a reliable content system and a fast, accessible web
experience. Avoid a complex platform architecture before usage justifies
it.

## 8.2 Suggested technical shape

A practical early stack could include: - Responsive web frontend. -
Content management system with structured content types. - Relational
database for place records and relationships. - Search index when native
database search is no longer sufficient. - Map provider with clear
licensing and cost controls. - Image storage and optimisation. -
Analytics with privacy-conscious configuration. - Automated backups and
error monitoring.

Possible implementation choices include a modern React-based framework,
a managed PostgreSQL database and a headless CMS. The exact vendors
should be selected after technical discovery, cost comparison, data
ownership review and team capability assessment.

Do not select technology because it is fashionable.

## 8.3 Core data entities

-   Place
-   Region / administrative area
-   Category
-   Story
-   Topic
-   Person or organisation (only where editorially justified)
-   Experience
-   Source
-   Media asset
-   Contributor
-   Editorial review
-   Correction
-   Relationship between records

This is a conceptual schema, not production-ready code. Validate fields,
permissions, data types and privacy rules before implementation.

## 8.5 Technical quality requirements

-   Mobile-first responsive layout.
-   Strong performance on ordinary mobile connections.
-   Optimised images and lazy loading.
-   Search-engine-friendly page structure.
-   Structured metadata where accurate.
-   Stable URLs.
-   Accessible semantic HTML.
-   HTTPS.
-   Role-based CMS permissions.
-   Regular backups and restore tests.
-   Logging and monitoring.
-   Spam and abuse protection.
-   Data minimisation and privacy controls.
-   Clear cookie and analytics policy.
-   Disaster recovery plan.

## 8.6 Languages

Recommended sequence: 1. English as the initial editorial production
language if the team can maintain quality. 2. Kinyarwanda as a priority
language for local accessibility and cultural relevance. 3. French as a
later expansion based on audience and capacity.

Do not launch machine-translated sensitive historical or cultural
material without qualified human review. Maintain a translation glossary
and editorial parity standards.

# 10. Trust, safety, legal and ethics

This platform handles cultural, historical, geographic and potentially
sensitive information. Trust must be designed into the business.

## 10.1 Editorial independence

-   Disclose paid content.
-   Separate advertising from editorial recommendations.
-   Never sell a "verified" label.
-   Do not let sponsors rewrite independent historical content.
-   Provide a transparent corrections channel.
-   Record conflicts of interest.

## 10.2 Privacy

-   Collect the minimum personal data required.
-   Explain why information is collected.
-   Obtain consent where required.
-   Protect contributor contact details.
-   Do not publish private community knowledge or personal details
    without an appropriate basis and consent.
-   Establish retention and deletion rules.
-   Review applicable Rwandan data-protection law and obtain qualified
    advice.

## 10.3 Intellectual property

-   Use properly licensed photographs, maps, text and archival material.
-   Keep licence records.
-   Obtain written permissions for commissioned work.
-   Attribute sources and creators.
-   Do not copy descriptions from tourism websites.
-   Do not assume publicly accessible means free to reuse.
-   Review map data and API terms.

## 10.4 Safety and visitor information

-   Do not give unsupported safety assurances.
-   Link to official authorities for current restrictions, park rules
    and emergency information.
-   Date time-sensitive visitor information.
-   Clearly distinguish editorial information from official
    instructions.
-   Avoid publishing sensitive access details for vulnerable cultural or
    ecological sites.

## 10.5 Governance

Before launch, obtain local legal advice on: - Business registration and
appropriate legal structure. - Data protection and privacy. - Consumer
protection if referrals or bookings are introduced. - Advertising and
sponsorship disclosure. - Copyright and licensing. - Tax and
invoicing. - Terms of use and liability. - Any permits or permissions
required for specific content gathering or commercial activity.

This document is strategic guidance, not legal advice.

# 11. Business model and financial discipline

## 11.1 Stage one: build trust

The first objective is not maximising revenue. It is proving that people
find the information useful and return to the platform.

Possible early funding: - Founder-funded lean operation. - Small
institutional or cultural grants. - Research and documentation
partnerships. - Sponsored projects with transparent editorial
boundaries. - Carefully selected philanthropic or educational support.

Do not build an expensive booking system before demand exists.

## 11.2 Stage two: commercial experiments

Potential revenue models: 1. Clearly labelled sponsored destination
guides. 2. Referral partnerships with responsible tour operators. 3.
Accommodation and experience referrals. 4. Downloadable themed guides.
5. Institutional research or content partnerships. 6. Educational
resources or licensing where appropriate. 7. Ethical advertising with
strict placement rules.

## 11.3 Stage three: platform services

Only after demand and operations are proven: - Trip-planning tools. -
Booking integrations. - Partner dashboards. - Destination data
services. - Institutional content partnerships. - Curated themed
itineraries.

## 11.4 Commercial guardrails

-   No pay-to-rank search results.
-   Sponsored content visibly labelled.
-   Editorial ratings only if a transparent methodology exists.
-   No invented user reviews.
-   No unverified claims from commercial partners.
-   No exclusive commercial arrangement that compromises public value.
-   No commission-based recommendation disguised as neutral advice.

## 11.5 Cost categories

Track: - Research and editorial labour. - Local contributor
compensation. - Photography and media rights. - Design and
development. - Hosting, database and map services. - Software and CMS. -
Translation and review. - Legal and accounting. - Marketing and
distribution. - Travel and field research. - Security, backups and
maintenance.

Set a monthly operating ceiling. Review actual cost per published and
maintained place, not just initial website build cost.

# 12. Go-to-market and distribution

## 12.1 Launch proposition

Do not announce "the complete guide to Rwanda" when the platform is only
beginning.

Use a truthful launch message:

**A growing atlas of Rwanda's places and stories. Explore the first
collection.**

## 12.2 Initial content campaign

Build a small editorial launch series: - 10 places that help explain
Rwanda. - A regional guide to Northern Rwanda. - Rwanda's important
museums and heritage sites. - Understanding the country's national
parks. - The landscapes of Lake Kivu. - Kigali beyond the usual
landmarks. - How to explore a place responsibly.

Each article should lead into structured place pages, not exist as an
isolated social post.

## 12.3 Distribution channels

Prioritise: - Search engines through useful, well-structured pages. -
Instagram for visual storytelling. - YouTube for carefully produced
short documentaries or explainers, if resources allow. - Email
newsletter only when there is a consistent editorial schedule. -
University, museum and cultural partnerships. - Local tourism and
community networks. - Relevant travel communities.

Avoid opening every social platform at once. Choose channels the team
can maintain.

## 12.4 Search strategy

Build pages around real information needs: - Place name + history. -
Place name + location. - What to know before visiting. - Museums in a
region. - Historical landmarks in Rwanda. - Nature and conservation
sites. - Things to explore near a known destination.

Search optimisation must follow content quality, not replace it. Do not
create hundreds of near-duplicate pages for search traffic.

# 13. Roadmap: foundation to scale

The following is a proposed sequence, not a fixed calendar. Each phase
should pass a clear evidence gate before the next begins.

## Phase 0 --- Validate the problem

**Objective:** prove that the product solves a real discovery and
knowledge problem.

Work: - Interview Rwandans, visitors, educators and tourism
practitioners. - Audit existing sources and platforms. - Identify
information gaps. - Test the working category and positioning. - Test
name territories. - Review content permissions and legal considerations.

Deliverables: - Research summary. - Validated audience priorities. -
Competitive landscape. - Initial content taxonomy. - Brand naming
shortlist. - Product requirements.

Gate: - At least two audience groups show a recurring need. - The team
can identify a clear gap beyond "one website with everything". - Initial
source and content workflows are feasible.

## Phase 1 --- Build the foundation

**Objective:** establish the identity, editorial system and minimum
technical architecture.

Work: - Finalise name and brand direction. - Define typography, colour,
logo and photography standards. - Create the place data model. - Set up
CMS and editorial permissions. - Create content templates. - Establish
sourcing and correction policy. - Build the initial design system.

Deliverables: - Brand guidelines. - Content handbook. - Data schema. -
CMS. - Responsive design system. - Initial prototype.

Gate: - A non-technical editor can create and update a place record. -
Reviewers can verify sources and approve publication. - The design is
accessible and works on mobile.

## Phase 2 --- Curated MVP

**Objective:** launch a useful, small but credible product.

Suggested content target: - 25--30 strong place profiles. - 8--12
substantial stories. - 4--5 regional collections. - One working map
experience. - Search and category navigation. - About, editorial
standards and correction pages.

Deliverables: - Public website. - Searchable place directory. - Place
profiles. - Editorial stories. - Map and filters. - Analytics and error
monitoring.

Gate: - Users can complete core discovery tasks. - Content quality is
reviewed. - No critical broken journeys. - Core pages meet accessibility
and performance checks.

## Phase 3 --- Prove usefulness

**Objective:** learn what people actually use.

Work: - Conduct usability sessions. - Review search terms and failed
searches. - Collect corrections and feedback. - Improve page
templates. - Add places based on demand and editorial capacity. - Test
repeat use and newsletter interest.

Gate: - Users can find and understand places without assistance. -
Search failures and usability issues are declining. - There is evidence
of repeat visits or meaningful referrals. - Editorial maintenance
remains manageable.

## Phase 4 --- Build authority

**Objective:** become a trusted reference in selected subject areas.

Work: - Expand geographic coverage. - Develop expert and institutional
review relationships. - Add Kinyarwanda content with human review. -
Publish richer historical and cultural explainers. - Improve
related-place connections. - Develop a consistent editorial series.

Gate: - Strong source coverage. - Sustainable content production. -
Demonstrable trust and useful external referrals. - No major unresolved
editorial or rights issues.

## Phase 5 --- Add utility and partnerships

**Objective:** make the platform more useful for people planning real
visits.

Possible additions: - Verified visitor information. - Official links and
booking referrals. - Themed exploration collections. - Downloadable
guides. - Partner profiles with disclosure. - Itinerary planning
experiments.

Gate: - Users request these services. - Partner information can be
maintained. - Commercial relationships do not compromise trust. - Unit
economics and support requirements are understood.

## Phase 6 --- Scale as a national platform

**Objective:** expand content, reach and institutional relevance without
diluting standards.

Work: - Build a contributor network across districts. - Establish formal
research and institutional partnerships. - Expand language coverage. -
Develop a richer knowledge graph. - Provide structured data or APIs
where justified. - Explore education and research applications. -
Consider regional expansion only after Rwanda coverage and quality are
strong.

Gate: - Governance is mature. - Funding supports editorial
maintenance. - Quality assurance scales with volume. - The platform
remains recognisably focused on Rwanda.

# 16. What not to build at launch

Do not build: - User accounts unless a real user need requires them. -
Public comments. - Open wiki editing. - Reviews and star ratings. -
Booking and payment infrastructure. - Live transport tracking. -
AI-generated historical answers without editorial controls. - A complex

gamification system. - A social feed. - A giant directory of
businesses. - Empty category pages. - A newsletter that has no
publishing schedule.

support and maintenance obligations.

# 20. Definition of a successful first version

The first version is successful when:

-   A user can find a known place quickly.
-   A user can discover an unfamiliar place through the map or
    collections.
-   A user can understand why a place matters.
-   A user can distinguish verified facts from missing or changing
    information.
-   A user can follow useful connections to other places and stories.
-   The editorial team can create, review, update and correct content
    without technical help.
-   The experience works well on mobile and with assistive technology.
-   The team can maintain the platform within its financial and
    operational limits.

It is not successful merely because the website looks beautiful or has
many pages.

# 21. Final strategic recommendation

Build **a knowledge institution expressed through a digital product**.

The website is the first interface, not the whole idea. The enduring
asset is the structured, trusted body of knowledge: place records,
historical context, geographic relationships, original stories, source
records, community partnerships and editorial standards.

If the website disappeared tomorrow, that knowledge system should still
have value and be portable.

The brand should therefore communicate calm authority, curiosity,
clarity and respect. Its design should be minimal because the
information deserves attention---not because minimalism is fashionable.

**The rule:** if a feature, colour, graphic, paragraph or interface
element does not improve understanding, discovery, trust or action,
remove it.

**Know the place. Understand the story.**

# Appendix D — Preliminary landscape observations (23 November 2026)

A first-pass review confirms that the category is not empty. Visit Rwanda already organises destinations by towns, national parks and other places, and also groups visitor interests such as responsible tourism, culture and heritage, and sport and adventure. Its destination coverage includes places such as Kigali, Musanze, Nyanza, Huye, Lake Kivu and Rwanda's national parks. See https://visitrwanda.com/tourism/destinations/ and https://visitrwanda.com/tourism/interests/. 

The national museums portal provides practical museum information, including opening hours and admission categories. This illustrates why the product should link to authoritative operators and verify time-sensitive visitor data rather than duplicate it without a maintenance process: https://www.rwandaheritage.gov.rw/en/visit-national-museums.

Other discovery-oriented websites also present Rwanda attractions and experiences. The existence of these services reinforces the need for differentiation through source transparency, deeper context, connected place records, local knowledge and a consistent editorial standard—not simply a larger list of attractions.

A useful market signal is that Rwanda's tourism sector has meaningful economic scale. The Rwanda Development Board reported tourism revenue and visitor figures for 2025 in a 2026 publication. Treat official figures as dated source claims and recheck the latest release before using them in public brand or investor materials: https://rdb.rw/rwanda-prepares-for-the-21st-kwita-izina-as-conservation-continues-to-drive-tourism-growth-and-community-development/.

**Strategic implication:** do not position the product as the official source of tourism information, or claim to be the first or only Rwanda destination guide. Position it as an independent, carefully edited knowledge and discovery layer that complements official destination services, museums, park operators, researchers and community sources.

This landscape note is preliminary. A full competitor audit should compare product scope, content depth, languages, search quality, geographic coverage, editorial sourcing, freshness, accessibility, mobile performance, ownership and business model.
