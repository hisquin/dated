### What Google’s own rules say \[S\]

- **Eligibility:** a business must make in-person contact with customers during its stated hours. Online-only businesses are not eligible. A service-area business that visits customers in person is eligible, and may hide its address.
- **One profile per business.** Duplicates can cause problems for both.
- **Names:** must reflect the real-world business name; other details go in the other fields.
- **Significant edits to name or category may be rejected.** If a business has changed significantly, Google says the previous business should be marked as permanently closed rather than renamed.
- **Description:** no links, no promotions or prices.

That explains what you hit. Changing “Educational consultant” to a software or design category looks to Google like turning a different business into another one, so it is flagged. The advice to create a new profile is consistent with the policy, but there is a trap: **a new profile while the old one stays live is a duplicate.**

### The decisions, in order

**Step 1. Check eligibility honestly.** Do you (a) have a physical studio or desk with set hours where people can meet you, or (b) visit clients in person (lodges, offices, founders in Musanze and the region)? If yes to either, Arkon qualifies. If neither, don’t keep a profile; use the website, directories and social instead. Your local plan (§09) is built on in-person visits, so (b) should be true.

**Step 2. Ask Google support first.** Use the Business Profile help contact option, quote the exact message you got and ask which route they require: unlock the category, or remove and recreate. This costs nothing and may unlock the category without losing the profile. Keep a record of what they say.

**Step 3. If the answer is “create a new one”:** retire the old profile first (use the remove or close flow support gives you), then create the new one. **Never run both live at the same time.**

**Step 4. About the 15 reviews.** A new profile starts at zero reviews. If those reviews came from real clients, that is a real loss; but you told me there are no real clients yet, so I have to be direct: reviews from people who were not customers (friends, family, the team) break Google’s review rules and can get a profile suspended. If any are not genuine, losing them is the safer outcome. Build the new review base from real customers only, asked after delivery, never bought, never traded for discounts.

## 05 · Market research

What I found, with sources in §15. Where I did not research something, I say so.

### Local demand: tourism and hospitality

- Rwanda’s tourism revenue reached about **$685 million in 2025** (a 6% rise), and visitors reached about **1.49 million** (from 1.36 million). \[S\]
- **Volcanoes National Park**, next to Musanze, generated **$35.8 million**, about **88%** of national-park revenue. \[S\]
- Domestic park visits rose about 15% in 2025, while international visits were flat. \[S\]
- Gorilla trekking is the largest single product; the government extended promotional permit rates through the end of 2026. \[S\]
- Musanze hosts events such as the annual gorilla-naming ceremony (in Kinigi), and a new eco-park is planned for the district. \[S\]
- New high-end and mid-range lodges keep opening around the park. \[S\]

**What it means \[I\]:** Musanze is the centre of Rwanda’s most valuable tourism product, and the businesses around it (lodges, guesthouses, operators, restaurants, guides) are many, close to you, and live or die by bookings. That is a rare, real, walkable market. **What I don’t know:** how many such businesses there are, how digitally mature they are, and what they’ll pay. The first research task is a list (§09, Strategy 1).

### Local demand: startups

- Kigali startups attracted roughly **$12 million** in funding in 2025, against about $30 million in Kampala, according to a report cited by a trade publication. \[S\]
- The Development Bank of Rwanda’s **Hanga Ventures Ignite** fund committed over €2 million to 28 startups since April 2025, in two grant tiers, aimed at ventures at ideation or MVP stage. \[S\]
- The programme behind it was scheduled to end in December 2026, so **check whether the window is still open or has been extended.** \[S\]
- An earlier Norrsken Kigali announcement mentioned seed cheques of $25,000 to $100,000; that is a 2019 article and may be outdated. \[S\]

**What it means \[I\]:** founders with grant money are real buyers of MVPs, but the amounts are small and often milestone-based. Local founder budgets will mostly sit in the low thousands.

### Competition density and price signals \[S\]

The Kigali listings I saw on Clutch and similar sites show many firms offering the same bundle (web, mobile, custom software). Self-reported hourly rates range from about **$25–$49** at the low end to **$100–$199** at the top, with stated project minimums from about **$1,000** to **$25,000+**. These are marketing listings, not audited prices.

### Global demand

**I did not research it.** My working assumption \[A\] is that founders and small businesses abroad do buy from Africa-based studios, but only after evidence of quality, which is why §03 and §11 of the research report put proof first.

### Forces that will shape the plan

- **AI coding tools make a basic prototype cheap \[K\].** Clients can now get a demo without you, so selling “a working app” is weaker. What you sell is judgement, reliability, local integration and ongoing care.
- **Local realities \[K, verify with clients\]:** mobile-first use, WhatsApp as a sales channel, mobile money (MTN MoMo, Airtel Money), multilingual users (English, French, Kinyarwanda), and variable connectivity. Products that handle these well are an advantage a foreign studio can’t easily copy.
- **Getting paid abroad is solved \[S\]:** Payoneer is widely used in Rwanda, with bank withdrawal to RWF; international clients generally can’t send USD straight to mobile money, so receive via Payoneer, Wise or a bank, then move to RWF. Whether **Stripe** works for a Rwanda-registered business, I did not verify; check before promising card checkout.
- **Rwandan domestic payments** are generally expected in RWF \[K\]; confirm with your accountant before invoicing local clients in dollars.

### A strategic option: lead with a vertical

Because the place is Musanze, the strongest wedge is **travel and hospitality**: local proof today, regional and global relevance later (Africa-focused travel startups and operators abroad also need booking and discovery products). My recommendation \[I\]: **spend about 70% of early effort on travel and hospitality** (local first, then regional, then global) and **30% on general founders**, then review at month 6. A specialist beats a generalist when you have no track record.

## 07 · Offers and pricing

### Principles

- **Fixed price, fixed scope, in writing.** No hourly billing.
- **Productise.** Build the booking module, site template and handover pack once and reuse them.
- **Founding rates are labelled and capped:** the first five projects or six months, whichever ends first, in exchange for a published record, a named testimonial and access to real outcome data.
- **Never discount without a trade.**
- **Show “from” prices.** Transparency is part of the positioning.
- **Deposits before work:** 50% up front for small jobs; 50/25/25 for larger ones.

### Effective hourly check \[A\]

If a Presence Site takes about 28 hours, a Booking Kit about 70, a Teardown about 16, a Prototype Sprint about 55 and an MVP about 180, your founding prices work out to roughly **$21–$32/hr for sites and kits, $25–$44/hr for teardowns, sprints and MVPs**. That is thin. It is acceptable for founding work **only if** the work produces records and reusable assets, and **only if** scope is held tight. By the third record, prices must rise.

### The binding constraint is capacity, not demand

Delivery hours for that mix (my estimate \[A\]): about 280 (sites) + 560 (kits) + 224 (teardowns) + 275 (sprints) + 720 (MVPs) + 200 (retainer) + 216 (care) + 100 (training, misc) = **about 2,600 hours**, before sales, admin and content, which add roughly another 25–30%.

A person can realistically deliver about 1,000–1,100 billable hours a year. **So the stretch case needs roughly three people at work, or two plus contractors from about month 5.** With two people, plan on the base case ($45k–$70k) and treat $100k as upside. ## 09 · Strategy playbooks, each with its counter-plan

Format for every strategy: **the play**, **how it runs**, **what to measure**, **what can go wrong**, **what to do if it does**. Fourteen are listed so you can choose; §11 says which to run when.

### S1. Local hospitality: Direct Booking Kit through field sales

- **Play:** walk into Musanze and Northern Province lodges, guesthouses, operators and restaurants with a free Site Report and a fixed-price offer.
- **How it runs:** (1) build a list of 100 businesses in week 1–2; (2) score each one’s site and booking path in 10 minutes; (3) send a WhatsApp message with a two-line finding and an offer to show it in person; (4) visit, show the report on a phone, quote a Presence Site or a Booking Kit; (5) deposit, build, launch; (6) ask for a Google review and a referral at day 14.
- **Measure:** reports sent per week, visits per week, proposals, wins, days from first visit to deposit.
- **Can go wrong:** owners can’t decide; budgets are smaller than your prices; everyone wants a “free website first”; payments arrive late; they want edits forever.
- **If it does:** lead with the Presence Site at a low fixed price and the Care Plan for continuity; require a 50% deposit; write a change-request rule into every quote; if price resistance is general, test a cheaper “starter page plus WhatsApp catalogue” offer before discounting the main product; if the whole segment is too slow, shift effort to S2 and S8.

### S2. Founding-client MVP programme

- **Play:** offer a capped number of founding slots to founders, in exchange for a record, testimonial and data.
- **How it runs:** publish the offer with its terms; reach founders via the ecosystem (S3) and the build log (S4); start with a paid Teardown; convert to a Prototype Sprint or MVP; contract the record and testimonial in advance.
- **Measure:** Teardowns sold, conversion to builds, margin per build, records published.
- **Can go wrong:** founders without money or clear problems; scope grows; the founder disappears; you under-price and burn out.
- **If it does:** screen hard (budget, decision-maker, a real user problem); keep scope in a written “in/out” list; use milestone payments; stop after the fifth founding slot even if it feels unfinished; no equity-for-work deals while you have no cash.

### S3. Ecosystem partnerships and referrals

- **Play:** become the studio that incubators, hubs, accountants and lawyers recommend.
- **How it runs:** list 15 organisations (for example hubs and programmes such as Norrsken Kigali and Impact Hub Kigali, funding programmes like Hanga, local business associations, accountants, lawyers, chambers); offer a free talk or a Teardown clinic; agree a simple referral thank-you; follow up monthly.
- **Measure:** meetings held, referrals received, warm introductions that become calls.
- **Can go wrong:** slow, relationship-heavy, and big hubs already have preferred vendors.
- **If it does:** keep doing the free clinic in public (it feeds S4 content); add smaller partners (accountants, lawyers, co-working spaces) who see founders earlier; don’t depend on one gatekeeper.

### S4. Public build log and content

- **Play:** make the work visible while it is happening.
- **How it runs:** a weekly build-log post (LinkedIn first, plus X/YouTube if you enjoy them); one essay a month (a real decision explained); a short video of a client visit or shipped feature; publish every record; link everything to the site.
- **Measure:** posts per week, inbound messages mentioning content, calls that came from content.
- **Can go wrong:** inconsistency; no audience for months; accidentally revealing client details.
- **If it does:** reduce to one post a week and one essay a month; ask clients before showing anything; write for a specific reader (a lodge owner, a first-time founder); if content stalls, rely on S1 and S3 until it compounds.

### S5. Your own product: a vertical booking and inquiry tool

- **Play:** turn the Direct Booking Kit into a reusable product for many operators, with a monthly fee.
- **How it runs:** deliver three kits by hand; extract the shared parts; launch a simple self-serve version with real users; charge a small monthly fee; keep a public changelog.
- **Measure:** active businesses, monthly revenue, support tickets per customer, churn.
- **Can go wrong:** low willingness to pay monthly; support eats your time; payment integrations are hard; no one uses it.
- **If it does:** keep it as an internal accelerator (it still cuts delivery hours); price it inside the Care Plan; do not hire for it; stop investing if fewer than 5 businesses are using it by month 9.

### S6. Marketplaces and directories for early cash

- **Play:** win small global jobs on platforms (Upwork, Contra and similar \[K\]) and build a presence on Clutch-style directories.
- **How it runs:** create strong profiles; apply daily to a narrow niche (travel and hospitality websites, MVP teardowns); use Payoneer for payouts \[S\].
- **Measure:** invitations, wins, effective hourly rate, repeat clients.
- **Can go wrong:** a race to the bottom; clients treat you as a commodity; platform fees; time wasted on bids.
- **If it does:** use it as a cash bridge only, capped at 20% of your time; put a floor price; steer good clients off-platform into your own offers where terms allow.

### S7. White-label and overflow work for agencies

- **Play:** be reliable extra capacity for agencies abroad or in Kigali.
- **How it runs:** list 30 small agencies; offer a paid pilot of one sprint; define day rates; deliver under their brand.
- **Measure:** pilots, repeat sprints, margin.
- **Can go wrong:** low margins, no credit, dependence on one agency, your brand stays invisible.
- **If it does:** cap at 25% of revenue; ask for case-study permission; stop if rates fall below your floor.

### S8. Outbound to travel and hospitality businesses and startups abroad

- **Play:** reach small African-safari operators, boutique lodges, and travel startups with a specific offer.
- **How it runs:** research 100 targets; send a personalised note with one observation about their booking path; offer a Teardown; follow up four times over three weeks.
- **Measure:** reply rate, calls booked, Teardowns sold.
- **Can go wrong:** low reply rates; trust gap because you have no references.
- **If it does:** lead with the public build log and the Site Report; add a risk-reducer (a small fixed-price Teardown); improve targeting before volume.

### S9. Workshops and cohorts

- **Play:** teach what you know (for example “Build a booking website,” “From idea to prototype”) to local businesses, students and founders.
- **How it runs:** a half-day workshop; an online cohort later; use the sessions to find clients.
- **Measure:** attendees, revenue, leads.
- **Can go wrong:** low prices, heavy prep, poor lead conversion.
- **If it does:** use workshops as a lead engine at a fair price; partner with a university or hub; stop if fewer than one client per workshop.

### S10. Google Business Profile, local search and WhatsApp

- **Play:** be found when people in Musanze search for web design and software.
- **How it runs:** fix the profile (§02); post weekly; collect real reviews; keep NAP (name, address or service area, phone) identical everywhere; WhatsApp Business with a catalogue.
- **Measure:** profile views, calls, direction or message actions, leads.
- **Can go wrong:** suspension, low search volume, poor review flow.
- **If it does:** follow the policy strictly; don’t chase reviews; if the profile is not eligible or fails, rely on S1 and S4.

### S11. Grants and competitions for your own product

- **Play:** use non-dilutive funding to build S5.
- **How it runs:** check eligibility for programmes such as Hanga Venture Ignite and Hanga PitchFest; prepare a one-page proposal built on a real, live product.
- **Measure:** applications, shortlists, awards.
- **Can go wrong:** eligibility rules (some require a registered business and traction), time spent on forms, programmes that end or change. One related programme was scheduled to end in December 2026. \[S\]
- **If it does:** treat any grant as a bonus; keep the plan funded by clients.

### S12. Events and community

- **Play:** be visible where buyers gather: pitch nights, hackathons, tourism fairs, chamber meetings, university events.
- **How it runs:** attend one event a month; give a short talk; always bring the capability sheet and a QR link to the Site Report offer.
- **Measure:** conversations, follow-ups, leads.
- **Can go wrong:** cost and time without leads.
- **If it does:** choose events by buyer density; cut those with no leads after two tries.

### S13. Institutions, NGOs and tenders (later)

- **Play:** conservation, health and education organisations in the north that need working systems.
- **How it runs:** partner with an established firm or a local NGO contact; start with a small paid pilot; document everything.
- **Measure:** pilots, contract value.
- **Can go wrong:** long cycles, delayed payments, heavy paperwork.
- **If it does:** avoid until cash reserves cover three months; use partners to carry the process burden.

### S14. Paid advertising (test only)

- **Play:** small tests on Google or social for the Site Report and Teardown offers.
- **How it runs:** after month 3 and only with a working landing page and tracking; budget small and capped.
- **Can go wrong:** wasted spend, wrong audience.
- **If it does:** stop after the cap and return to organic and referral channels.

## 10 · The client acquisition system: discovery, booking, delivery

One process for every client, so it is repeatable and improvable.

### The pipeline

| # | Stage | What happens | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Target** | Build and score a list (S1, S8) | A weekly list of 25 |
| 2 | **Reach** | Personalised message; follow-ups at day 3, 7, 14 | Replies |
| 3 | **Qualify** | 5 quick questions: need, decision-maker, budget range, timing, readiness | Go / no-go |
| 4 | **Discovery call** (30 min) | Understand the problem; show the process; discuss price range | Notes and next step |
| 5 | **Book** | Calendar link; short intake form; confirmation message | Call booked |
| 6 | **Proposal** | 1–2 pages: problem, scope in and out, deliverables, timeline, price, payment schedule, assumptions | Sent within 48 hours |
| 7 | **Contract and deposit** | Agreement plus statement of work; deposit invoice | Work starts only after both |
| 8 | **Kickoff** | Access, goals, success measure, schedule, one decision-maker | Written kickoff note |
| 9 | **Delivery** | Weekly demo, Friday written update, tracked change requests | Visible progress |
| 10 | **Launch and handover** | Launch checklist, documentation, 30-day support | Live product |
| 11 | **Review and record** | Retrospective; metrics with their method; build record drafted; testimonial and review asked | Proof |
| 12 | **Aftercare and referral** | Care Plan offer; referral ask at day 30 and 60 | Recurring revenue and referrals |

### Scripts (adapt the wording; have native speakers review local-language versions)

**Local first message (WhatsApp):**

> Hello \[name\], I’m \[your name\] from Arkon, a product studio here in Musanze. I looked at \[business\]’s website on my phone and noticed \[one specific, true observation\]. We made a short free report with three quick fixes. May I show it to you this week, in person or on a call?

**Founder message (LinkedIn or email):**

> Hi \[name\], I read about \[their product\]. I’m \[your name\], founder at Arkon Studio, a small product studio in Musanze. I had one question about \[specific part of their flow\]. We offer a fixed-price Product Teardown (three to five days) that turns an idea into a scoped plan. Happy to share a sample first. Would that help?

**Follow-up rhythm:** day 3 (a useful link, not “just checking in”); day 7 (one new observation); day 14 (a polite close: “I’ll leave it here; the offer stands”).

### Discovery call agenda (30 minutes)

1. **Five minutes:** what they’re building and why now.
2. **Ten minutes:** the problem, the users, what has been tried, what “done” would look like.
3. **Five minutes:** your process: fixed scope, weekly demo, written record.
4. **Five minutes:** timing and budget range, plainly.
5. **Five minutes:** next step and date.

**Questions worth asking:** who decides, and what happens if nothing is built; how are bookings or customers handled now; what is the one number that would tell you this worked; what must not break; who will use it every day.

**Red flags:** no budget and no urgency; “just a simple app” with unlimited features; wants equity instead of payment; wants to see work before paying for any; no single decision-maker; asks you to copy someone else’s product. Walk away or reduce to a paid Teardown.

### Proposal and contract musts

- **Proposal:** problem in their words; scope in and out; deliverables; timeline; price; payment schedule; assumptions; what happens if scope changes.
- **Contract:** parties; scope and deliverables; schedule; price and milestone payments; change-request process; IP transfers on final payment; right to show the work in your portfolio; confidentiality; termination and kill fee; support period; governing law. **Have a local lawyer review your template once.** I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice.

### Delivery rules

- **One weekly demo, always**, with a short written update after it.
- **Single decision-maker** on the client side.
- **Change requests** logged, priced and approved in writing.
- **Definition of done** for each deliverable agreed up front.
- **Reply within 24 hours** on working days; say so in the contract.
- **Handover pack:** documentation, logins transferred, how-to video, support terms.

### Tools (low cost; check current prices yourself)

Calendar and booking (Cal.com or similar); CRM and tasks (Notion or Airtable); design (Figma); code (GitHub); invoicing (a free or low-cost invoicing tool); payments (Payoneer, bank transfer, mobile money for local clients \[S\]); contract signing (any e-signature tool); analytics (Search Console plus a simple privacy-friendly option).

### The numbers to review every Friday

New touches; conversations; proposals sent; proposals won; revenue booked; cash collected; hours spent per project against estimate; posts published; one record advanced.

## 11 · Scaling, timelines and decision gates

### The four phases

| Phase | When | Goal | What you do | Revenue target (stretch) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **0. Reset and foundation** | Days 0–14 | A clean, honest, findable studio | Section 01 clean-up; domain, email, profile route; identity kit v1; offers, contract and proposal templates; target lists | $0 |
| **1. Prove** | Days 15–90 | First paying clients and first records | S1 field sales, S3 partnerships, S4 build log; 3 to 5 paid projects; first Teardown | about $6k cumulative |
| **2. Systemise** | Months 4–6 | Repeatable delivery and rising prices | Two published records; reusable booking module; first MVP; first Care Plans; raise prices; start S8 outbound | about $25k cumulative |
| **3. Scale** | Months 7–12 | Capacity and a steady pipeline | Second and third builders (contract or hire); retainers; selective ads and events; product test (S5) | about $100k cumulative |

### Decision gates: what to check and what to do

A gate is a date, a number and a pre-agreed response. Agree them now, while you are calm.

| Gate | Check | Green | Amber | Red: what to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **G1, day 30** | Paid projects signed; real conversations | 1+ signed or $1k collected; 30+ conversations | Conversations good, nothing signed | **Red:** under 15 conversations means volume is the problem (raise outreach to 40 a week). Over 30 conversations and zero signed means the offer or price is wrong: test a cheaper entry offer (Presence Site or Teardown) |
| **G2, day 60** | Cash collected | $2.5k+ | $1k–$2.5k | **Red:** move to the cash-bridge mode (S6, S7) at up to 30% of your time while keeping S1 and S4 running |
| **G3, day 90** | Cumulative cash; records | $6k+ and two records drafted | $3k–$6k | **Red:** hold a one-day review: which segment answered, which offer sold; kill the weakest strategy and double the strongest |
| **G4, month 6** | Cumulative cash; recurring revenue | $25k+ and $500+ per month recurring | $12k–$25k | **Red (under $8k):** switch to the “plan Z” in §12 |
| **G5, month 9** | Monthly run-rate | $10k+ per month | $6k–$10k | **Red:** stop new fixed costs; concentrate on the best-converting offer; raise prices on it |
| **G6, month 12** | Annual total; margin; backlog | $95k+ with 2 months of backlog | $50k–$95k | **Red:** reassess strategy against the downside case; decide on year two with the data |

### Hiring and capacity rules

- **Hire only against signed work:** first contractor when run-rate has held at about $6k per month for two months and utilisation has stayed above 80% for six weeks.
- **Order:** an engineer or designer to match your gap, then a second builder, then part-time sales or partnership help on commission.
- **Pay per deliverable** at first; convert to a salary only when the pipeline covers six months.
- **Keep fixed costs under 40% of revenue.**
- **Train locally:** universities, hubs and bootcamps around Musanze and Kigali are a likely source of junior talent \[K\]; pair juniors with a senior and document the process.
- **Founder time:** about 40% on selling in months 1–3, falling to about 25% in months 7–12.

### Operating rhythm

| Cadence | What |
| --- | --- |
| **Daily** | 2 hours outreach and follow-up; delivery blocks; 30 minutes content |
| **Weekly** | Monday planning; Friday demo, update and numbers review |
| **Monthly** | Review each strategy’s results; one essay; one event; update the pipeline and cash forecast |
| **Quarterly** | Prices; offers; gates; hiring |

### Resilience basics \[K\]

A backup internet connection; a power bank or UPS; code, files and designs backed up off-site; shared accounts held by the company, not individuals; written handover notes for each project so any builder can step in.

### After $100k (a short look ahead)

If the year goes well, year two choices are: raise prices and shrink the client list; hire a second delivery lane; develop the product into a real recurring line; take a second vertical; build the Academy. Don’t plan these in detail until G5 is green.

### Plan Z: the pre-agreed fallback

If at G4 (month 6) cumulative cash is under about $8k and the pipeline holds less than about $5k of likely work, **don’t quit and don’t pretend.** Switch to a bridge mode, agreed in advance: about half your time on paying work (white-label for agencies, platform gigs or part-time employment), the other half on the build log, one product test and the best-performing local offer. Keep the brand honest and small, and review again at month 9. A studio that survives to year two honestly is worth more than one that burns out chasing a number.
