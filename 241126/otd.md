# 0. Executive strategy

## 0.1 The central proposition

**Rwanda Atlas is a national knowledge, discovery and travel platform that helps people understand Rwanda, discover meaningful places and experiences, and plan how to explore them.**

It connects three equally important product pillars:

1. **KNOWLEDGE — Understand Rwanda.**  
   Learn about the country's geography, history, heritage, culture, communities, environment and identity through reliable, contextual information.

2. **DISCOVERY — Find Rwanda.**  
   Explore places, landscapes, attractions, experiences, regions and stories through a connected geographic system.

3. **TRAVEL — Experience Rwanda.**  
   Access useful, verified visitor information, discover experiences and local services, and find practical ways to plan and arrange visits.

These are not three unrelated products placed under one website. They are three connected ways of engaging with the same country.

Knowledge gives places meaning. Discovery helps people find them. Travel helps people experience them.

## 0.2 The organising principle

**Geography connects everything.**

The primary organising object remains the place, not the hotel, tour or tourist attraction.

A place can connect to:
- Its location and surrounding geography.
- Its history and cultural significance.
- Its environment and natural features.
- People, communities and organisations.
- Stories and educational resources.
- Activities and experiences.
- Visitor information and relevant services.
- Nearby places and suggested exploration.

This makes Rwanda Atlas more than a tourism directory. It becomes a connected information system about Rwanda.

## 0.3 Positioning statement

For Rwandans and international visitors who want to understand, discover or explore Rwanda, Rwanda Atlas is a trusted digital platform that connects the country's places with their stories, geography, culture, experiences and practical visitor information.

Unlike a conventional tourism directory, travel agency or general encyclopedia, Rwanda Atlas brings knowledge, geographical discovery and practical travel planning together in one carefully edited experience.

## 0.4 Brand promise

Every meaningful interaction should help users do at least one of three things:

- Understand something meaningful about Rwanda.
- Discover a place, story or experience they might otherwise miss.
- Make a better-informed decision about exploring Rwanda.

Where possible, a destination page should help users accomplish all three.

## 0.5 Working tagline

**Know Rwanda. Discover more. Explore deeper.**

Alternative lines to test:
- Rwanda, understood and explored.
- Discover the places. Understand the stories.
- A deeper way to experience Rwanda.

The tagline remains subject to audience testing and name clearance.

# 1. Foundation and opportunity

## 1.1 The problem

Information about Rwanda is spread across tourism websites, government portals, museums, park operators, accommodation providers, travel agencies, publications, academic sources, local organisations and social platforms.

Users often have to visit several sources to understand one destination or organise a visit.

The opportunity is to connect information that is usually separated.

Rwanda Atlas addresses six related problems:

1. **Fragmented knowledge:** information about places, history, culture and geography is scattered.
2. **Shallow destination descriptions:** places are often presented as attractions without enough explanation of their significance.
3. **Uneven discovery:** many places, local experiences and regional stories are difficult to find.
4. **Disconnected travel information:** visitors may struggle to find reliable practical details alongside meaningful destination context.
5. **Weak geographic connections:** destinations, nearby places, experiences and stories are rarely connected in one coherent system.
6. **Trust and currency:** historical claims require careful sourcing, while visitor information can become outdated.

These are product hypotheses that require validation through interviews, research, content audits and prototype testing. They should not be presented as proven market findings.

## 1.2 The opportunity

Build a platform where a person can:

- Learn about Rwanda without planning a trip.
- Find meaningful places to visit within a particular region.
- Understand the history or cultural significance of a destination.
- Discover experiences beyond familiar tourist routes.
- Compare relevant experiences and practical options.
- Find verified visitor information and official links.
- Discover responsible local operators and services.
- Follow connections between places, stories and experiences.
- Research Rwanda for education, work or personal interest.

## 1.3 Mission

Make reliable knowledge about Rwanda easier to discover, understand and use, while helping people experience the country more meaningfully.

## 1.4 Vision

Become one of the most trusted and useful independent digital platforms for understanding, discovering and exploring Rwanda.

## 1.5 Purpose

To document Rwanda's places and stories, make its geography and heritage accessible, connect people with meaningful experiences, and support informed exploration of the country.

## 1.6 Product principles

1. **Truth before traffic.** Never trade accuracy for clicks.
2. **Useful before abundant.** A smaller verified collection is better than a large, weak directory.
3. **Context before promotion.** Explain why a place matters before promoting a visit.
4. **Discovery without manipulation.** Help people find places through relevance, geography and editorial judgement—not paid ranking.
5. **Local knowledge matters.** Work with people and institutions connected to the places being documented.
6. **Respect is non-negotiable.** Handle history, identity, culture and sensitive sites responsibly.
7. **No false completeness.** Clearly communicate what is known, unknown, unavailable or awaiting verification.
8. **Editorial independence.** Commercial relationships must never masquerade as independent recommendations.
9. **Accessibility by default.** Design for mobile devices, different bandwidth conditions and varied abilities.
10. **Build sustainably.** Every feature must justify its development and maintenance costs.

# 2. Audience and positioning

## 2.1 Primary audience: Rwandans

People living in Rwanda who want to:
- Learn more about their country.
- Discover places to visit during weekends, holidays or short breaks.
- Understand local history, heritage and geography.
- Find interesting activities within their own districts or nearby regions.
- Discover places beyond the destinations they already know.
- Access useful information before visiting a location.

The product should never assume that Rwandans already know their country's places, history or experiences.

## 2.2 Primary audience: International visitors

People planning or experiencing a visit to Rwanda who want to:
- Understand the country before arriving.
- Discover destinations and experiences.
- Learn about cultural and historical context.
- Explore beyond conventional travel itineraries.
- Find practical information about access, timing and visitor requirements.
- Identify relevant accommodation, guides, operators and experiences.
- Make more informed travel decisions.

International visitors need clarity, confidence and practical usefulness. They should not have to sacrifice cultural depth to get travel information.

## 2.3 Secondary audiences

**Rwanda's diaspora:** People with personal, cultural or family connections who want to reconnect with the country.

**Students and educators:** People seeking structured, sourced information about Rwanda's geography, history, culture and heritage.

**Researchers and practitioners:** Journalists, tourism professionals, cultural organisations, conservation groups and researchers seeking a useful starting point.

**Local businesses and experience providers:** Organisations that may eventually use the platform to reach relevant audiences through transparent commercial partnerships.

## 2.4 Shared audience needs

The product must work for both primary audiences without assuming that every user is a tourist.

A Rwandan exploring a weekend destination and an international visitor researching a cultural site should be able to use the same underlying place information, while seeing the practical details relevant to their needs.

## 2.5 Positioning boundaries

Rwanda Atlas is:
- A national place-based knowledge platform.
- A geographic discovery experience.
- An editorial publication.
- A practical travel research and planning resource.
- A curated connection between destinations and relevant experiences.
- A long-term digital knowledge asset.

It is not:
- A conventional travel agency.
- A hotel booking marketplace at launch.
- A general business directory.
- A social network.
- An open-edit encyclopedia.
- A paid-ranking platform.
- An official government service unless formally authorised.
- A replacement for official authorities, park operators or emergency services.

# 3. Brand strategy

## 3.1 Brand essence

**Rwanda, understood and explored through place.**

## 3.2 Brand character

Rwanda Atlas should feel:

- Informed, not unnecessarily academic.
- Curious, not sensational.
- Calm, not cold.
- Welcoming, not overly familiar.
- Proud of Rwanda, without exaggeration or propaganda.
- Helpful, without overwhelming users.
- Credible, without pretending to know everything.
- Commercially sustainable, without feeling commercial-first.

## 3.3 Personality boundaries

| Be | Avoid |
|---|---|
| Clear | Corporate jargon |
| Curious | Clickbait |
| Credible | Unsupported superlatives |
| Human | Forced friendliness |
| Editorial | Tourist-brochure clichés |
| Respectful | Exoticising communities |
| Useful | Feature overload |
| Independent | Paid recommendations disguised as neutral advice |

## 3.4 Voice and tone

**Default voice:** clear, intelligent, warm, concise and evidence-aware.

Use plain English first. Preserve meaningful Rwandan names and terms. Explain unfamiliar concepts without removing their cultural context.

Distinguish documented facts from interpretation. Use precise dates and locations. Do not invent practical visitor information to make a page look complete.

Avoid generic tourism expressions such as "hidden paradise", "magical destination" and "untouched gem" unless the language is genuinely justified.

Never treat communities as attractions or assume that one person's experience represents an entire community.

## 3.5 Naming strategy

Rwanda Atlas remains the working name, not a cleared final brand.

Before public launch, check:
- Trademark and company-name conflicts.
- Domain availability.
- Social media handles.
- Linguistic meaning and pronunciation.
- Suitability for local and international audiences.
- Whether the name could imply official government status.

The name should be suitable for a knowledge, discovery and travel platform—not just a tour operator.

# 4. Brand identity direction

## 4.1 Creative direction

**Editorial cartography.**

The identity should combine:
- The precision of geographic information.
- The clarity of a modern reference publication.
- The warmth of documentary photography.
- The practicality of a field guide.
- The editorial depth of a cultural journal.

The identity should feel credible and distinctive without becoming a generic safari brand, luxury hotel identity or technology startup aesthetic.

## 4.2 Logo direction

Explore three routes during a separate identity-design process:

**A. Wordmark first:** a distinctive typographic wordmark.

**B. Cartographic mark:** an abstract mark inspired by coordinates, contours, map folds or connected locations.

**C. Editorial monogram:** a restrained initial-based mark supported by a strong wordmark.

Requirements:
- Works in one colour.
- Remains legible at small sizes.
- Works in black and white.
- Avoids unnecessary gradients.
- Avoids generic safari and tourism symbols.
- Does not depend on a tagline to be recognisable.

No final logo should be selected before the creative exploration and evaluation process.

## 4.3 Colour system

Keep the palette restrained and functional. Use one principal brand accent rather than assigning a different decorative colour to every category.

The original provisional palette can remain a starting point for testing:

| Token | Suggested value | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Ink | `#202522` | Primary text |
| Paper | `#F7F6F1` | Main background |
| White | `#FFFFFF` | Content surfaces |
| Forest | `#315B49` | Main brand accent |
| Muted | `#777D76` | Secondary text |
| Line | `#E4E3DC` | Dividers and borders |
| Alert | `#A34D3E` | Semantic warnings only |

These are not final approved brand colours. Test contrast, readability and visual coherence before adoption.

Knowledge, discovery and travel should be distinguished through information architecture, typography, labels and content—not through three competing accent palettes.

## 4.4 Typography and layout

Use a restrained one- or two-family type system.

Explore readable editorial serif and highly legible sans-serif combinations. Ensure that all chosen fonts support Kinyarwanda characters and required language expansion.

Design principles:
- Strong typographic hierarchy.
- Comfortable reading widths.
- Consistent spacing.
- Wide layouts for maps and visual discovery.
- Minimal decorative shadows and gradients.
- Avoid excessive rounded cards.
- Clear metadata and location labels.
- Responsive mobile-first behaviour.

## 4.5 Photography and media

Use authentic, location-specific documentary photography.

- Show people with dignity and appropriate consent.
- Credit photographers and respect licensing.
- Record image location, date, author and rights where possible.
- Do not imply an image depicts a specific place when its location is uncertain.
- Use archival images, maps and diagrams only with appropriate permissions.
- Never use AI-generated images as evidence of a real place, event, artefact or person.
- Clearly label illustrative imagery.

# 5. Product architecture

## 5.1 The three product pillars

### Pillar 1 — Knowledge: Understand Rwanda

This pillar provides the reliable information behind the platform.

Content areas:
- History.
- Geography.
- Cultural heritage.
- Traditions and contemporary culture.
- Nature and biodiversity.
- Conservation.
- Communities and regional context.
- Historical events and landmarks.
- Glossary and reference topics.

The objective is not to reproduce an entire encyclopedia. It is to provide useful, contextual information connected to real places and credible sources.

### Pillar 2 — Discovery: Find Rwanda

This pillar helps users explore the country.

Content areas:
- Places and destinations.
- District and regional collections.
- Natural and cultural attractions.
- Museums and heritage sites.
- Activities and experiences.
- Nearby places.
- Thematic collections.
- Interactive map and geographic browsing.
- Editorial recommendations and curated exploration routes.

Discovery should work even when a user does not know what they are looking for.

### Pillar 3 — Travel: Experience Rwanda

This pillar helps people move from interest to informed action.

Content areas:
- Practical destination information.
- Access and location.
- Opening hours and admission fees when verified.
- Official destination links.
- Experiences and activities.
- Accommodation discovery.
- Responsible tour operators and local guides.
- Transport and transfer information where relevant.
- Visitor guidance and responsible travel information.
- External booking and enquiry links.

The platform should initially direct users to trusted external providers rather than attempt to own the entire booking process.

## 5.2 How the pillars connect

The experience should feel like one continuous journey:

**Learn → Discover → Plan → Experience → Learn more.**

Example:

A user reads about Rwanda's royal history.

They discover a relevant historical place.

They explore its location and nearby destinations.

They read about its cultural and geographical context.

They check verified visitor information.

They follow an official link or contact a relevant experience provider.

After exploring the destination, they can discover related places and stories.

This connected journey is the product's core advantage.

## 5.3 Primary navigation

Recommended top-level navigation:

1. **Explore** — places, regions, categories, experiences and map.
2. **Stories** — editorial narratives, explainers and documentary content.
3. **Knowledge** — structured reference topics, history, culture, nature and geography.
4. **Plan a Visit** — practical travel information, experiences and relevant services.
5. **About** — mission, editorial policy, sources, corrections and contact.

Search should be globally accessible.

Unlike the original version, Plan a Visit should be a first-class navigation destination because travel is now an equal product pillar. Its initial content can remain curated and limited; it should not become an empty directory.

## 5.4 Geographic taxonomy

Geography remains the organising backbone.

Use a controlled data structure:
- Country.
- Province or City of Kigali.
- District.
- Sector.
- Cell or village where appropriate and publicly suitable.
- Coordinates.
- Alternate and local names.

Some visitor-facing locations may cross administrative boundaries or be known by different names. Store alternate names and geographic information rather than forcing every place into an inaccurate structure.

## 5.5 Content taxonomy

Initial top-level categories:

- Nature and wildlife.
- History and heritage.
- Culture and community.
- Museums and memorials.
- Cities and places.
- Experiences and activities.
- Accommodation and stays.
- Food and local experiences.

Categories may overlap. Every place should have one primary category and may have multiple relevant secondary tags.

Commercial categories must not dominate the knowledge taxonomy.

## 5.6 Search

Search should support:
- Place names and alternative names.
- Districts and regions.
- Topics and categories.
- Story titles.
- Experiences and activities.
- Relevant travel information.
- Common spelling variations.
- English and Kinyarwanda terms, with other languages as capacity grows.

Results should explain why an item matched.

## 5.7 Map experience

The map is a core discovery tool.

MVP requirements:
- Pan and zoom.
- Place markers.
- Search and filters.
- Category and region browsing.
- Place previews.
- Links to complete profiles.
- A list alternative for accessibility.
- Clear map-data attribution.
- A useful fallback when map tiles fail or bandwidth is limited.

Do not build live navigation, route optimisation or complex trip routing in the first release. Link to trusted mapping services where useful.

- Relevant activities.
- Important features.

- Location and coordinates.
- How to access the destination, where verified.
- Opening hours.
- Admission fees.

Where appropriate:
- Local guides.
- Responsible tour operators.

- Transport or transfer providers.

- Correction link.

**A destination profile must never become an advertisement disguised as an editorial page.**

The editorial description should remain independent. Commercial information belongs in a clearly identified section.

# 7. Content and editorial strategy

## 7.1 Content pillars

The editorial system should support five connected content types:

1. **Places:** structured records of locations.
2. **Stories:** narratives and explainers that give places context.
3. **Knowledge:** reference topics and educational resources.
4. **Travel guidance:** practical information for informed exploration.
5. **Connections:** related destinations, themes, experiences and geographic clusters.

## 7.2 Source hierarchy

Prioritise:
1. Primary sources and official records.
2. Relevant public institutions and site operators.
3. Peer-reviewed research and reputable academic publications.
4. Museums, conservation organisations and cultural institutions.
5. Reputable journalism and specialist publications.
6. First-hand interviews and community knowledge, properly attributed and contextualised.

An official source is not automatically complete or neutral. Use judgement, corroboration and context.

Maintain a source record for every important factual claim.

## 7.3 Historical and sensitive content

Rwanda's history requires exceptional care, particularly the 1994 Genocide against the Tutsi.

The platform must:
- Use historically accurate and respectful terminology.
- Consult recognised institutional and scholarly sources.
- Avoid sensational imagery.
- Distinguish evidence, testimony, interpretation and memory.
- Consult qualified reviewers where necessary.
- Provide appropriate context and content notices.
- Avoid unmoderated public edits to sensitive material.
- Correct errors transparently.

## 7.4 Cultural content

- Do not present cultural practices as frozen or uniform.
- Explain regional, generational and community variation.
- Seek community review for sensitive or sacred knowledge.
- Respect restrictions on private or culturally sensitive material.
- Do not publish knowledge simply because it can be collected.
- Attribute community contributions appropriately.

## 7.5 Travel information verification

Travel information requires a separate maintenance standard because it changes more frequently than historical and geographical context.

For time-sensitive information, record:
- Information supplied.
- Source or official provider.
- Date checked.
- Person responsible for verification.
- Next review date.
- Any relevant limitation or uncertainty.

Do not publish unverified opening hours, fees, availability, access conditions or safety assurances.

If information is unavailable, say so.

## 7.6 Content production workflow

Topic selection → Research → Source collection → Draft → Fact-checking → Local or specialist review → Editorial approval → Publication → Scheduled review → Correction or update.

## 7.7 Community contributions

At launch, accept corrections and suggestions through a moderated form or email.

Do not introduce unrestricted public publishing.

Provide a clear policy for:
- Consent.
- Attribution.
- Contributor rights.
- Sensitive information.
- Corrections.
- Removal requests.
- Privacy.

Home → Knowledge → Topic → Related places → Sources.

A user should be able to learn about Rwanda without being pushed into travel services.

## 8.2 Journey B — Discover

Home → Explore → Map or category → Place preview → Place profile → Related destinations.

The experience should encourage meaningful exploration rather than endless browsing.

Explore → Experience category → Experience profile → Location and context → Provider information → External enquiry or booking.

Search → Place result → Destination profile → Knowledge, discovery and travel information.

1. Brand and clear navigation.
2. Search.
3. Editorially selected destination or story.

5. Selected destinations and experiences.

7. A clear invitation to plan a visit.

# 9. Travel features and commercial services

The travel pillar should grow in stages.

## 9.1 Initial travel features

Include:
- Destination visitor information.
- Official links.
- Selected experience profiles.
- Regional exploration collections.
- Relevant accommodation and operator links where verified.
- Responsible travel guidance.
- External booking or enquiry links.

These features can exist without a booking engine or a large partner directory.

## 9.2 Later travel features

Only after demand is demonstrated:
- Curated itineraries.
- Downloadable regional travel guides.
- Accommodation referral integrations.
- Tour and experience booking integrations.
- Trip-planning tools.
- Partner profile management.
- Structured destination information services.

## 9.3 What not to build immediately

Do not launch with:
- A complex booking engine.
- Payment processing for third-party services.
- Live transport tracking.
- A full hotel marketplace.
- A large unverified operator directory.
- User reviews and star ratings.
- A complex itinerary builder.
- Partner dashboards without demonstrated need.

The platform should first prove that people use it to understand, discover and plan.

# 10. Business model and monetisation

## 10.1 Commercial philosophy

Rwanda Atlas should generate revenue from the value it creates—not from compromising the credibility of its information.

The platform's commercial model should support its editorial mission.

**Users should trust the platform before being asked to buy through it.**

## 10.2 Stage 1 — Establish value and audience

Primary objective: build a credible product and prove recurring usefulness.

Potential funding:
- Lean founder-funded operation.
- Relevant grants and institutional support.
- Research and documentation partnerships.
- Sponsored projects with strict editorial boundaries.
- Educational and cultural partnerships.

At this stage, prioritise useful content, search visibility and audience trust over aggressive monetisation.

## 10.3 Stage 2 — Introduce commercial revenue

Potential revenue streams:

**1. Sponsored destination guides**  
A tourism organisation or relevant business can sponsor a clearly labelled guide. The editorial content remains under Rwanda Atlas control.

**2. Tour and experience referrals**  
Earn a referral fee or agreed commission when users enquire about or book eligible experiences through a partner.

**3. Accommodation referrals**  
Refer users to relevant accommodation providers through trackable links or agreed referral arrangements.

**4. Featured partner profiles**  
Offer clearly labelled commercial profiles for responsible operators, guides, accommodation providers and experience businesses.

**5. Downloadable travel guides**  
Sell carefully produced regional guides, themed travel collections and practical exploration resources.

**6. Institutional research and content partnerships**  
Provide commissioned destination documentation, structured information and editorial resources for appropriate institutions.

**7. Ethical advertising**  
Allow carefully selected advertising only where it does not undermine the experience or editorial standards.

## 10.4 Stage 3 — Develop platform services

After traffic, partner demand and operating capacity are proven:

- Booking integrations.
- Curated trip-planning services.
- Partner tools and dashboards.
- Destination data services.
- Institutional content licensing.
- Premium research and travel resources.
- More advanced travel discovery features.

## 10.5 Commercial guardrails

These rules are non-negotiable:

- No pay-to-rank search results.
- Sponsored content must be visibly labelled.
- Never sell a verified label.
- No invented user reviews.
- No unverified claims supplied by commercial partners.
- Commission-based recommendations must not appear neutral.
- Commercial partners cannot rewrite independent historical or cultural content.
- No exclusive commercial arrangement that compromises public value.
- Organic discovery must remain editorially controlled.

## 10.6 Revenue priority

The most sensible early commercial focus is:

1. Relevant tour and experience referrals.
2. Accommodation referrals.
3. Sponsored regional or destination guides.
4. Downloadable travel guides.
5. Selected institutional partnerships.

Do not build a large advertising business before the platform has a meaningful audience.

## 10.7 Financial discipline

Track:
- Research and editorial costs.
- Contributor compensation.
- Photography and media rights.
- Development and maintenance.
- Hosting, CMS and map services.
- Translation and specialist review.
- Legal and accounting costs.
- Marketing and distribution.
- Customer and partner support.

Set a monthly operating ceiling. Track the cost of maintaining content, not only the cost of building the website.

# 11. Technology and data foundations

## 11.1 Build philosophy

Build a reliable, maintainable web product around structured information.

The website should be the first interface to a larger knowledge system.

## 11.2 Suggested technical structure

- Responsive web frontend.
- Structured CMS.
- Relational database.
- Geographic data and map integration.
- Search.
- Image storage and optimisation.
- Privacy-conscious analytics.
- Backups and error monitoring.
- Role-based editorial permissions.

Select vendors after comparing cost, reliability, ownership, licensing and team capability.

## 11.3 Core data entities

- Place.
- Region and administrative area.
- Category.
- Story.
- Knowledge topic.
- Experience.
- Provider or organisation.
- Accommodation.
- Visitor information record.
- Source.
- Media asset.
- Contributor.
- Editorial review.
- Correction.
- Commercial relationship.
- Relationship between records.

Commercial records must not be mixed with editorial source records.

## 11.4 Place data model

Each place should support:
- Stable record ID and URL.
- Preferred and alternate names.
- Place type and category.
- Geographic coordinates.
- Administrative location.
- Summary and descriptive content.
- Historical, cultural and environmental context.
- Experiences and activities.
- Visitor information.
- Official links.
- Related places and stories.
- Sources and media credits.
- Editorial status.
- Verification and review dates.

Visitor information should have its own verification status and review schedule.

## 11.5 Language strategy

Recommended sequence:
1. English as the initial production language if editorial quality can be maintained.
2. Kinyarwanda as a priority language for local accessibility and cultural relevance.
3. French as a later expansion based on audience demand and capacity.

Design the technical system for multilingual content from the beginning.

Do not publish machine-translated sensitive historical or cultural material without qualified human review.

# 12. Operating model and governance

## 12.1 Operating principle

The most important operational asset is the repeatable system for producing and maintaining trustworthy information.

## 12.2 Lean launch team

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Founder / product lead | Strategy, priorities, partnerships, budget |
| Managing editor / researcher | Editorial standards, research and publishing |
| Local contributors | Local context, leads, interviews and corrections |
| Designer / developer | UX, build, brand system and maintenance |
| Specialist reviewers | Historical, cultural, ecological or legal review |

Some roles can be fractional or contracted at launch.

## 12.3 Editorial governance

Establish:
- Editorial ownership and approval rights.
- Sensitive-content escalation.
- Source standards.
- Correction policy.
- Conflict-of-interest policy.
- Sponsorship disclosure.
- Contributor agreements.
- Image and intellectual-property permissions.
- Privacy and takedown procedures.

## 12.4 Partnerships

Potential partners include:
- Museums and heritage institutions.
- Universities and researchers.
- Tourism bodies.
- Park managers and conservation organisations.
- Local historians and cultural practitioners.
- Community organisations.
- Responsible tour operators.
- Accommodation providers.
- Local guides and experience providers.
- Photographers and documentary storytellers.

A partnership should improve accuracy, access, context, experience or distribution. A logo exchange alone is not meaningful.

## 12.5 Independence from commercial partners

If Rwanda Atlas has an ownership or commercial relationship with a travel operator, accommodation provider or related business, that relationship must be disclosed wherever relevant.

The platform must not quietly prioritise affiliated businesses in editorial recommendations or search results.

This separation is especially important if the founder has other tourism interests.

# 13. Trust, safety, legal and ethics

## 13.1 Editorial independence

- Disclose paid content.
- Separate advertising from editorial recommendations.
- Never sell a verified label.
- Do not let sponsors control independent historical content.
- Maintain a corrections channel.
- Record conflicts of interest.

## 13.2 Privacy

- Collect only necessary personal information.
- Explain why information is collected.
- Protect contributor contact details.
- Obtain consent where required.
- Establish retention and deletion rules.
- Review applicable Rwandan data-protection requirements with qualified advisers.

## 13.3 Intellectual property

- Use properly licensed photographs, maps, text and archival material.
- Maintain licence records.
- Obtain written permissions for commissioned work.
- Attribute sources and creators.
- Do not copy descriptions from tourism websites.
- Review map-data and API terms.

## 13.4 Visitor information and safety

- Do not give unsupported safety assurances.
- Link to official authorities for current restrictions and emergency information.
- Date time-sensitive information.
- Distinguish editorial information from official instructions.
- Avoid publishing sensitive access details for vulnerable cultural or ecological sites.

Obtain local legal advice before introducing referrals, bookings, advertising or commercial travel services.

# Education Platform
## Product Strategy & Brand Direction
**Strategic foundation — Version 1.0**

## 1. Strategic vision

Build a trusted digital platform that makes education opportunities easier to discover, understand, access and act upon.

The platform will help Rwandan students, graduates and young professionals find relevant opportunities locally and internationally, understand the pathways available to them, and make informed decisions about their educational and professional futures.

**Vision:** A world where access to educational opportunities is not limited by a person's ability to find information.

**Mission:** To connect people with meaningful educational opportunities and provide the information and guidance they need to pursue them confidently.

## 2. The problem we are solving

Educational opportunities exist, but discovering and pursuing them can be unnecessarily difficult.

Potential users face several challenges:

- Opportunities are scattered across university websites, government portals, social media, institutional announcements and other platforms.
- Important deadlines and eligibility requirements are easy to miss.
- Scholarship announcements often lack clear explanations of who qualifies and what funding covers.
- Students may struggle to identify universities, courses or programmes that match their qualifications and ambitions.
- Application instructions can be confusing, particularly for first-time applicants.
- Some opportunities are outdated, misleading or difficult to verify.
- Students often do not know what to pursue after completing secondary school, university or professional training.

The platform should reduce this fragmentation and uncertainty.

It should answer five essential questions:

1. What opportunities are available?
2. Which opportunities are relevant to me?
3. Am I eligible?
4. What do I need to apply?
5. What should I do next?

## 3. Primary audience

### Primary audience: Rwandan students and graduates

The initial audience includes:

**Secondary school students**
- University admission opportunities.
- Scholarships and financial assistance.
- Local and international university programmes.
- Career and study-pathway information.

**University students**
- Scholarships for further study.
- Exchange programmes.
- Fellowships and academic programmes.
- Internships and research opportunities.
- Professional courses and certifications.

**University graduates**
- Postgraduate scholarships.
- Master's and PhD opportunities.
- Professional development programmes.
- Fellowships, internships and graduate programmes.
- Career-transition and skills-development pathways.

**Young professionals**
- Short courses and certifications.
- Professional fellowships.
- Leadership programmes.
- Career advancement opportunities.
- International learning experiences.

### Secondary audiences

- African students seeking educational opportunities.
- Universities and higher education institutions.
- Scholarship providers and foundations.
- Training institutions and online learning providers.
- Employers and organisations offering fellowships, internships and graduate programmes.
- Government and development organisations.

The initial product should serve Rwandan users exceptionally well before expanding its country-specific coverage across Africa.

## 4. Product positioning

The platform is an **education discovery and opportunity intelligence platform**.

It is not simply:
- A scholarship announcement page.
- A university directory.
- An online course marketplace.
- A job board.
- A general education blog.

It brings these information categories together through a structured discovery and guidance experience.

**Core value proposition:**

Find the right opportunity. Understand your options. Know your next step.

The product should combine opportunity discovery, reliable information, eligibility guidance and educational pathways.

## 5. Product pillars

The platform will have five interconnected pillars.

### Pillar 1: Scholarships and funding

Help users discover financial support for education.

Content includes:
- Undergraduate scholarships.
- Master's scholarships.
- PhD scholarships.
- Fully funded scholarships.
- Partially funded scholarships.
- Government scholarships.
- University-funded scholarships.
- Research grants.
- Education bursaries and financial aid.

Every opportunity should explain its funding coverage, eligibility, application requirements, deadlines and application process where the information is available.

### Pillar 2: Universities and academic programmes

Help users discover where and what they can study.

Content includes:
- Universities in Rwanda.
- Universities across Africa.
- International universities.
- Undergraduate programmes.
- Master's programmes.
- Doctoral programmes.
- Exchange and mobility programmes.
- Academic admission opportunities.
- Distance and online learning programmes.

University and programme information should be structured so users can compare meaningful details rather than simply browse institution names.

### Pillar 3: Courses and professional development

Help users develop relevant knowledge and skills.

Content includes:
- Online courses.
- Professional certifications.
- Technical and vocational training.
- Digital skills programmes.
- Short courses.
- Leadership and entrepreneurship programmes.
- Language programmes.
- Industry-specific training.

Where possible, identify the provider, duration, delivery format, cost, certification and prerequisites.

### Pillar 4: Fellowships, internships and development programmes

Help users access opportunities that connect education with practical experience.

Content includes:
- Academic fellowships.
- Research fellowships.
- Professional fellowships.
- Internships.
- Graduate programmes.
- Leadership programmes.
- Exchange programmes.
- Entrepreneurship and innovation programmes.
- Youth development programmes.

### Pillar 5: Career and educational pathways

This pillar is essential to differentiation.

Instead of only publishing individual opportunities, help users understand possible routes to their goals.

Examples:
- How to progress from secondary school to university.
- How to prepare for a Master's scholarship.
- How to pursue a PhD.
- How to study abroad.
- How to enter a particular profession.
- Which certifications can support a career transition.
- How to prepare a competitive scholarship application.

This turns the platform from an information listing service into a practical educational guide.

Help users avoid missing opportunities.

- Upcoming deadlines.

- Scholarship CVs.

- Your guide to finding a fully funded Master's scholarship.

Introduce accounts when there is enough value to justify them.

Potential features:
- Saved opportunities.
- Personal education profile.
- Deadline reminders.
- Personalised recommendations.

## 9. Trust and credibility strategy

Trust should be a defining product feature.

**Source hierarchy**

2. Official university or institutional website.

- Link to original sources.

- Correct inaccurate information promptly.
- Disclose sponsored content.

- Progress and direction.
- International relevance.

The identity should work equally well for a scholarship listing, a university profile, a career guide and a professional development opportunity.

Avoid predictable education-brand clichés such as graduation caps, generic open books, trophy imagery and excessive motivational graphics.

Do not finalise a logo or colour palette until the name and creative direction have been properly explored.

## 20. Naming principles

The brand name should:
- Be distinctive and memorable.
- Work in Rwanda and internationally.
- Be easy to pronounce and spell.
- Avoid limiting the platform to scholarships alone.
- Allow future expansion into education, skills and career pathways.
- Feel credible to students, universities and institutional partners.
- Be suitable for a digital platform.

The name should not sound like a government programme, a single scholarship fund or a university.

Potential naming territories should be explored before choosing a final name.

## 21. Brand architecture

Keep the main platform as one coherent brand.

Its categories—Scholarships, Universities, Courses, Fellowships, Career Pathways and Guidance—should operate as product sections rather than separate brands.

This makes the platform easier to understand and avoids unnecessary fragmentation.

## 22. Long-term ambition

The long-term ambition is to become a trusted education and opportunity infrastructure platform for Rwanda, with the potential to serve a wider African audience.

The platform should evolve from helping people discover opportunities to helping them navigate educational and professional pathways.

The ultimate goal is not to publish the most opportunities.

It is to help more people identify the right opportunities, understand their options and take meaningful next steps.

*2 - Understand*

# LOCAL VALUE PLATFORM

# PART I — THE BIG IDEA

## 1. Vision

Build the most useful local value and benefits discovery platform in Rwanda, helping people access better value from the businesses, services, brands and programmes already around them.

The platform should become part of people's everyday decision-making.

Before deciding where to eat, shop, exercise, relax, travel locally or spend money on services, users should have a reason to check the platform.

## 2. Mission

Make existing value easier to discover and access, while helping local businesses attract customers through relevant offers and measurable customer engagement.

## 3. The problem

Consumers miss opportunities to save or receive additional value because offers are fragmented.

They may exist:
- On a restaurant's Instagram page.
- On a supermarket's social media account.
- On a bank's cardholder benefits page.
- Through a mobile network promotion.
- Inside a loyalty programme.
- On a business website.
- Through an employer or membership organisation.
- In a WhatsApp group.
- On a printed promotion inside a shop.
- Through a direct business announcement.

People have to know where to look, remember what they have access to and understand how to redeem it.

Businesses also face a problem:
- Their promotions do not always reach the right people.
- They depend heavily on social media algorithms.
- They struggle to measure whether promotions bring customers.
- They may offer discounts without a clear acquisition or retention strategy.
- Their existing benefits and promotions can remain underused.

The platform should connect these two problems.

## 4. The central product proposition

**One place to discover what is available, what you qualify for and where you can get more value.**

The platform should combine:

1. Existing public offers.
2. Existing membership and loyalty benefits.
3. Existing bank, telecom and institutional promotions.
4. New local merchant deals.
5. Curated experiences and value packages.

It should not require every business to invent a new discount or become a full partner before its existing offers can be discovered.

That is an important strategic difference.

## 5. The strategic positioning

A local value and benefits discovery platform.

Not simply:
- A coupon website.
- A restaurant discount application.
- A merchant directory.
- A loyalty card.
- An advertising platform.
- A group-buying website.
- A marketplace that requires every purchase to happen inside its own system.

The product sits between consumers and the value already available in the local economy.

**Positioning statement:**

For people who want to get more value from their everyday spending, the platform brings together local deals, existing offers, membership benefits and useful promotions in one accessible place.

For businesses and organisations, it creates an additional channel to reach customers, communicate offers and measure engagement.

# PART II — PRODUCT STRATEGY

## 6. The four product pillars

### Pillar 1: Discover

Help people find value around them.

Examples:
- Restaurant offers.
- Coffee and dining promotions.
- Shopping discounts.
- Supermarket promotions.
- Beauty and personal care offers.
- Fitness and wellness benefits.
- Entertainment and events.
- Travel and leisure promotions.
- Courses and professional services.
- Family activities.

Discovery should work by category, location, occasion, budget and availability.

### Pillar 2: Access

This is the most important differentiating pillar.

Help users discover benefits that already exist outside the platform.

Potential sources:
- Bank cardholder offers.
- Mobile network promotions.
- Existing business loyalty programmes.
- Membership benefits.
- Employer benefits.
- Student benefits.
- Partner organisations.
- Publicly advertised promotions.
- Official brand campaigns.

Example:

A person might already qualify for a discount through their bank card but does not know the benefit exists.

The platform explains:
- Which bank or card qualifies.
- Which merchant participates.
- What benefit is available.
- Any restrictions.
- How to redeem it.
- Where the official terms can be found.

Another example:

A mobile subscriber may qualify for a promotion from their network. The platform can make that promotion discoverable and direct them to the official activation process.

The platform should become an access guide to existing value.

Important: access to an offer does not mean the platform owns it. Every third-party offer must retain its original terms and source, and any integration or redistribution must be authorised.

### Pillar 3: Unlock

Help local businesses create new reasons for customers to visit.

Offer types can include:
- Percentage discounts.
- Fixed-price promotions.
- Buy-one-get-one offers.
- Free add-ons.
- Bundled services.
- Off-peak promotions.
- First-visit offers.
- Loyalty rewards.
- Limited-time experiences.
- Special packages.

Do not make price reductions the only form of value.

A restaurant might offer a complimentary drink. A gym might offer a free introductory session. A salon might bundle two services. A hotel might offer a weekend package.

The objective is to help customers feel they received something worthwhile while helping businesses generate profitable demand.

### Pillar 4: Experience

Help users turn discovery into action.

A user should be able to:
- Understand the offer.
- Check whether they qualify.
- See its location.
- Understand the redemption process.
- Save it.
- Share it.
- Redeem it or access the official provider.
- Record the benefit received where verification is supported.

The platform should reduce the distance between seeing an offer and actually using it.

## 7. The key product concept: a Value Access Layer

The platform should organise offers by how users access them.

### A. Public offers

Available to anyone.

Examples:
- A restaurant promotion advertised publicly.
- A supermarket sale.
- An event discount.
- A public holiday promotion.

Access method: visit the merchant, follow the official link or use the displayed code.

### B. Partner offers

Created directly with a participating business.

Examples:
- An exclusive introductory package.
- A special customer offer.
- A limited-time promotion.

Access method: platform code, QR code, redemption link or merchant confirmation.

### C. Existing membership benefits

Available to people who already belong to a programme.

Examples:
- Loyalty card benefits.
- Club memberships.
- Gym member privileges.
- Student benefits.
- Employer benefits.

Access method: present the qualifying membership or account.

### D. Financial and telecom benefits

Offers from banks, mobile networks and payment providers.

Examples:
- Cardholder promotions.
- Mobile money campaigns.
- Subscriber offers.
- Payment-linked discounts.

Access method: official activation, qualifying payment method or provider instructions.

### E. Curated value packages

Offers assembled around a need or occasion.

Examples:
- A date-night package.
- A weekend in Kigali.
- A family activity day.
- A self-care package.
- A student essentials collection.

These packages should combine verified existing offers or separately agreed partner offers. Do not imply that unrelated merchants have entered a joint agreement when they have not.

## 9. The offer profile

Every offer should have a structured profile.

Required information:
- Offer title.
- Merchant or issuing organisation.
- Category.
- Location or service area.
- Offer description.
- Normal price, if verified.
- Promotional price, if applicable.

- Eligibility requirements.
- Start and end dates.
- Availability restrictions.
- Redemption instructions.
- Official source or partner confirmation.
- Last verification date.
- Current status.

Information published by the original provider.

**Merchant confirmed**
The business has directly confirmed the offer.

**Partner exclusive**
A promotion created or authorised for the platform.

A user has reported the offer, but the provider has not yet confirmed it.

# PART III — THE DIFFERENTIATION STRATEGY

## 10. How we avoid becoming another deals platform

The competitive advantage should not be the number of discounts published.

It should be the usefulness of the access layer.

### Differentiator 1: Bring existing offers into one discovery experience

Most users do not want to search five social media accounts, three websites and several membership programmes.

Create a structured index of existing value.

This should include offers from businesses and institutions, subject to permission, source and terms.

### Differentiator 2: Show users what they qualify for

A user should be able to create a simple benefits profile.

For example:
- I am a student.
- I have a particular bank card.
- I subscribe to a particular mobile network.
- I belong to a gym or loyalty programme.

The platform can then surface relevant benefits.

Do not collect sensitive financial information or bank credentials. A user should never need to disclose a PIN, password or full payment-card number to discover a promotion.

### Differentiator 3: Measure actual value received

Build a personal record of benefits used.

Users should see:
- Offers accessed.
- Confirmed savings.
- Benefits redeemed.
- Businesses visited.
- Value received over time.

Only record savings when the discount or benefit can be reasonably verified. Do not treat an advertised maximum discount as a user's actual saving.

### Differentiator 4: Help users decide where to spend

Do not only display the biggest discount.

Help users discover value based on:
- Their location.
- Their interests.
- Their budget.
- Their occasion.
- Their eligibility.
- Their preferred businesses.

### Differentiator 5: Help businesses create profitable offers

The platform should provide businesses with guidance on:
- Which customers they want to attract.
- What offer they should run.
- When to run it.
- What redemption conditions to use.
- How to measure results.

This turns the business relationship into more than paying for a listing.

# PART IV — THE HABIT AND DISTRIBUTION ENGINE

## 11. The biggest challenge: getting people to use it

The platform must not depend on users remembering that a website exists.

It needs to become useful in existing daily routines.

### A. The daily value check

Create a daily editorial product:

**Today's Value**

A short selection of genuinely useful offers and benefits.

Examples:
- A useful lunch offer.
- A shopping promotion.
- A local activity.
- A benefit available to qualifying subscribers.
- A service offer that expires soon.

Distribute it through:
- WhatsApp opt-in broadcasts.
- Website and mobile web.
- Email digest.
- Social media.
- Partner business channels.

Quality is more important than publishing a large number of offers.

### B. The weekly value guide

Publish a recurring weekly collection:

- What is worth doing this weekend?
- Where can students get value this week?
- Best family activities.
- Useful offers for working professionals.
- New local experiences.
- Benefits expiring this month.

This gives people a reason to return even when they are not searching for a specific discount.

### C. Location-based discovery

Create useful local collections.

Examples:
- Best value around Kimihurura.
- Offers near Kigali Heights.
- Affordable lunch options around town.
- Weekend activities in Kigali.
- Useful services around Remera.

Start with one concentrated market area rather than trying to cover every part of Rwanda immediately.

### D. The QR distribution network

Place a simple QR code at participating businesses.

It should allow customers to:
- Discover the business's current offers.
- See other offers nearby.
- Access existing benefits.
- Follow the business.
- Share an offer.

The QR code should create a reason to interact with the platform at the exact moment a customer is spending money.

### E. The community distribution network

Recruit a small network of ambassadors.

Potential communities:
- Universities.
- Offices.
- Residential communities.
- Fitness and wellness communities.
- Professional networks.
- Student associations.

Ambassadors should distribute useful value guides and bring real users, not simply generate social media impressions.

### F. The referral loop

Make useful offers easy to share.

A user shares an offer with a friend. The friend accesses it, visits the merchant and discovers the platform.

The reward should only be issued after a meaningful action, where possible, to discourage fake referrals.

## 12. The repeat-use mechanism

The platform needs several reasons for repeat visits.

| User need | Product response |
|---|---|
| I want to save money today | Today's Value |
| I want to go somewhere | Nearby offers |
| I want to shop | Existing and new retail promotions |
| I want to use my membership | My Benefits |
| I want something to do this weekend | Weekly Value Guide |
| I do not want to miss an offer | Expiry reminders |
| I want to see whether I saved | My Savings |
| I want to share something useful | Shareable offer pages |

The goal is to build a repeatable behaviour:

**Check → Discover → Access → Use → Save → Return.**

# PART V — GO-TO-MARKET

## 13. Initial market

Recommended launch hypothesis: Kigali first.

Kigali offers a practical starting environment for testing merchant density, consumer behaviour and offer distribution. This is a strategic recommendation, not a claim that every category or neighbourhood has already been validated.

Start with a narrow group of consumers:

- Students and young professionals.
- Value-conscious working adults.
- People who regularly spend on food, shopping, personal services and entertainment.

Expand to other customer groups once the product demonstrates repeat use.

## 14. Initial categories

Do not launch every category at once.

Prioritise:

1. Food and dining.
2. Beauty and wellness.
3. Shopping.
4. Fitness and recreation.
5. Entertainment and experiences.

These categories create opportunities for frequent consumer interactions.

Then expand into:
- Supermarkets.
- Household services.
- Education and courses.
- Travel and leisure.
- Professional services.
- Family services.

## 15. Supply-side acquisition

Recruit businesses that already have something valuable to offer.

Start with:
- Independent restaurants and cafés.
- Salons and spas.
- Gyms and wellness studios.
- Retail stores.
- Event organisers.
- Local experience providers.

Approach businesses with a practical offer:

"We help people discover the value you already provide, bring you additional customers and show you which promotions generate real engagement."

Do not begin by demanding a monthly subscription.

### Merchant acquisition sequence

1. Identify businesses with an existing customer base.
2. Find their current promotions and public benefits.
3. Ask permission to feature the offer.
4. Agree on accurate terms and redemption instructions.
5. Publish a free basic profile or approved offer.
6. Track interest and redemptions.
7. Show the merchant the results.
8. Introduce paid services when measurable value exists.

The first objective is useful offer inventory, not merchant subscription revenue.

## 16. The first 90 days

### Days 1–15: Validation

- Interview 30–40 potential consumers.
- Interview 15–20 local businesses.
- Identify existing offer sources.
- Understand current redemption behaviour.
- Test whether people want discovery, existing benefits or new deals most.
- Select a concentrated launch area.

### Days 16–30: Build the supply foundation

- Recruit approximately 20 anchor businesses.
- Identify at least 10 legitimate existing offer sources.
- Secure permission where required.
- Collect accurate offer information.
- Establish verification and expiry processes.
- Build a basic mobile-friendly web experience.

These are initial operating targets, not market facts.

### Days 31–60: Controlled launch

- Launch to a small community.
- Distribute the daily value guide.
- Place QR codes at participating merchants.
- Run a weekly value campaign.
- Measure clicks, visits and redemptions.
- Interview users after they try offers.

### Days 61–90: Prove repeat behaviour

- Improve categories and discovery.
- Remove offers that are no longer useful.
- Add more existing benefits.
- Develop merchant reporting.
- Introduce referral mechanics.
- Identify the strongest recurring use case.

At day 90, decide whether the platform has earned a larger investment.

# PART IX — TRUST, GOVERNANCE AND RISK

## 30. Consumer trust

- Show offer terms clearly.
- Identify the source.
- Distinguish official and partner offers.
- Show expiry dates.
- Provide a way to report incorrect offers.
- Do not overstate savings.
- Do not hide exclusions.
- Do not sell user data.
- Obtain consent for marketing notifications.

## 31. Merchant trust

- Agree on redemption terms.
- Avoid unauthorised discount promises.
- Provide clear campaign reporting.
- Explain commercial fees.
- Label sponsored placement.
- Respect merchant pricing and brand identity.

## 32. Critical risks

**Stale offers:** Build expiry and verification processes into the product.

**Fake discounts:** Require transparent reference prices and accurate terms.

**Low consumer adoption:** Distribute through existing channels and physical merchant touchpoints.

**Merchant fatigue:** Demonstrate actual customer value before asking for subscriptions.

**Low offer density:** Launch in a concentrated area and a few categories.

**Third-party access restrictions:** Secure permission and official links instead of assuming every existing promotion can be republished or redeemed through the platform.

**Unprofitable redemption economics:** Measure the value to both merchant and customer, not just the size of a discount.

# PART X — SUCCESS AND LONG-TERM AMBITION

## 33. North-star metric

**Verified value actions per active user per month.**

A value action could be:
- An offer redeemed.
- A benefit successfully accessed.
- A qualified referral completed.
- A confirmed merchant visit resulting from a platform offer.

The platform should not count every page view as a success.

## 34. Supporting metrics

Consumer:
- Monthly active users.
- Repeat usage.
- Saved offers.
- Offer redemption rate.
- Benefits accessed.
- Confirmed savings.
- Referral activity.

Merchant:
- Active merchants.
- Active offers.
- Qualified customer visits.
- Verified redemptions.
- Repeat merchant participation.
- Merchant retention.

Operational:
- Percentage of active offers with current verification.
- Expired offer rate.
- Incorrect offer reports.
- Time required to verify an offer.

## 35. Long-term ambition

Become a trusted local value and benefits infrastructure platform for Rwanda, with the potential to expand into other African markets.

The platform should eventually help people navigate not only discounts, but the full range of benefits available through local businesses, financial institutions, brands, employers and membership organisations.

The ultimate ambition is not to have the largest list of discounts.

It is to become the place people check because they know it helps them get more value from everyday life.
